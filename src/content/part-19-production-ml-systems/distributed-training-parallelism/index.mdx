<Hook>
  One GPU can't hold a modern model, let alone train it. So the model and its data get split across many
  GPUs — but "split" is not one operation. Split the batch, split the weight matrices, or split the layers,
  and you get three different training runs with three very different amounts of chatter between machines.
</Hook>

<Intuition>
  <IntuitionDemo />

  The same tiny 2-layer model, the same 4 GPUs. Switch strategies and watch what actually gets sent over the
  network change — not just how much of it, but what kind of thing it even is.
</Intuition>

<Formalize>
  All three strategies eventually bottom out in a **ring all-reduce**: $P$ GPUs arranged in a ring, each
  sending and receiving $(P-1)/P$ of a payload during a reduce-scatter pass, then again during an all-gather
  pass, to end with every GPU holding the same combined result:

  $$
  V_{\text{ring}}(s, P) = \frac{2(P-1)}{P}\,s
  $$

  <Variables>
    <Variable>**$s$** — the size of the payload being combined across GPUs.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$P$** — the number of GPUs.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$V_{\text{ring}}$** — total communication volume for one ring all-reduce of that payload.</Variable>
  </Variables>

  The three strategies differ only in *what* payload goes through this operation, and *how often*:

  $$
  V_{\text{data}} = V_{\text{ring}}(\text{params}, P) \qquad V_{\text{tensor}} = 2L \cdot V_{\text{ring}}(H, P) \qquad V_{\text{pipeline}} = 2H(L-1)
  $$

  <Variables>
    <Variable>**$\text{params}$** — the model's total parameter count (every GPU holds a full copy).</Variable>
    <Variable>**$H$** — the hidden dimension of one layer's activation.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$L$** — the number of layers.</Variable>
  </Variables>

  <Steps>
    <Step label="Data parallel: sync the whole gradient">
      Every GPU holds the entire model and computes on its own slice of the batch, so what has to sync is
      the full gradient — one ring all-reduce over all `params`, every single step.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Tensor parallel: sync one activation, at every layer">
      Weights are sharded across GPUs (no gradient sync needed at all), but each layer boundary needs its
      partial activations combined — a much smaller payload ($H$, not `params`), paid $2L$ times per step
      (forward and backward, at every layer).
    </Step>
    <Step label="Pipeline parallel: no collective, ever">
      Layers are assigned whole to different GPUs. Only the boundary between adjacent stages needs an
      activation handed across — point-to-point, not a ring all-reduce — so it never pays the $2(P-1)/P$
      collective overhead at all.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</Formalize>

<Play>
  <PlayDemo />

  Grow the cluster and data-parallel volume grows with it, since $2(P-1)/P \to 2$ as $P$ climbs — more GPUs
  means more ring hops. Tensor-parallel volume grows the same way, just against a much smaller payload.
  Pipeline-parallel volume doesn't move at all: it was never a function of $P$ in the first place.
</Play>

<WorkedExample>
  <Steps>
    <Step label="The model: 2 layers, 4×4 each, 32 parameters total, 4 GPUs">
      $\text{params} = 4 \times 4 \times 2 = 32$. Ring all-reduce factor at $P=4$: $2(4-1)/4 = 1.5$.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Data parallel">
      $V_{\text{data}} = 1.5 \times 32 = 48$ — the entire gradient, moved once.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Tensor parallel">
      Per boundary: $1.5 \times 4 = 6$. Two boundaries per layer (forward + backward) × 2 layers: $6 \times 2 \times 2 = 24$.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Pipeline parallel">
      Only $L - 1 = 1$ inter-layer boundary, forward and backward: $4 \times 1 \times 2 = 8$ — the least of
      the three, because it never pays the ring all-reduce factor at all.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</WorkedExample>

<Summary>
  $$
  V_{\text{data}} = \frac{2(P-1)}{P}\cdot\text{params}
  $$

  None of the three strategies is "the" way to distribute training — they're different trades. Data
  parallelism is simple and scales the batch, but every GPU needs the whole model to fit in memory and the
  gradient sync grows with parameter count. Tensor parallelism shrinks the per-GPU memory footprint but pays
  a communication tax at every single layer. Pipeline parallelism communicates the least, but idles GPUs
  during the fill-and-drain of its pipeline unless the batch is sliced into enough microbatches to keep it
  full. Real large-model training (Megatron-LM, DeepSpeed, GPT-NeoX) combines all three at once, tuned to
  the cluster's actual network topology. Having built and trained something, the next chapter turns to
  serving it: finding the nearest match to a query among millions of stored vectors, without comparing
  against every one.
</Summary>
