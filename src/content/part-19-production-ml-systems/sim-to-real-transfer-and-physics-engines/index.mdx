<Hook>
  A policy trained entirely in a physics engine like MuJoCo or Isaac Gym never has to fight friction it
  didn't expect, backlash in a gearbox, or a battery that delivers a little less torque than the spec
  sheet promised. The real robot does. That mismatch is the reality gap, and it doesn't need a full
  physics simulator to demonstrate — a controller that assumes it's getting 100% of its commanded motion,
  meeting an actuator that only delivers 60%, shows the exact same failure mode.
</Hook>

<Intuition>
  <IntuitionDemo />

  Same gain, same target, same number of control steps. In sim the arrow lands on the target dot. In
  "real," with the actuator delivering less motion per command than assumed, the same gain undershoots —
  not because anything is broken, just because the model the controller was tuned against wasn't quite
  right.
</Intuition>

<Formalize>
  A proportional controller commands motion equal to gain times error, but the actuator only delivers a
  fraction of what's commanded. That turns the position error into a geometric sequence:

  $$
  e_T = e_0 \,\bigl|1 - f\,K\bigr|^{\,T}
  $$

  <Variables>
    <Variable>**$e_T$** — the remaining distance to the target after $T$ control steps.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$e_0$** — the starting distance to the target.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$f$** — the friction factor: the fraction of commanded motion the actuator actually delivers ($f=1$ is the sim's assumption).</Variable>
    <Variable>**$K$** — the controller's proportional gain.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$T$** — the fixed number of control steps before checking success.</Variable>
  </Variables>

  <Steps>
    <Step label="Sim assumes f = 1">
      A gain tuned so that $|1-K|$ is small enough to converge within $T$ steps in sim is really only
      tuned for $f=1$ — it has no reason to also work for a smaller $f$.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Domain randomization means tuning against a range of f, not one value">
      Training against $f=1$ produces a controller that's brittle to exactly one number. Training against
      many sampled $f$ values (0.6, 0.8, 1.0, ...) produces a gain that keeps $e_T$ small across the whole
      range — the real robot's actual $f$ just needs to be somewhere in that trained range.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</Formalize>

<Play>
  <PlayDemo />

  Identical gain, identical target, identical step budget — only the friction changed, and that alone
  flips the outcome from success to failure.
</Play>

<WorkedExample>
  <Steps>
    <Step label="Sim: f=1, K=0.5, four steps">
      $e_4 = 4 \cdot |1 - 1(0.5)|^4 = 4 \cdot 0.5^4 = 4(0.0625) = 0.25$ — comfortably inside the 0.5
      tolerance.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Real: same gain, f=0.6">
      $e_4 = 4 \cdot |1 - 0.6(0.5)|^4 = 4 \cdot 0.7^4 = 4(0.2401) = 0.9604$ — the exact same controller,
      the exact same math, now fails the tolerance.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Compensating for the known friction closes the gap">
      Solving $4|1-0.6K|^4 \le 0.5$ for $K$: divide both sides by 4 to get $|1-0.6K|^4 \le 0.125$; take the
      fourth root, $|1-0.6K| \le 0.125^{1/4} \approx 0.595$; so $1-0.6K \le 0.595$, giving
      $K \ge (1-0.595)/0.6 \approx 0.676$. Any gain past roughly $K \approx 0.68$ brings the real robot back
      under tolerance in the same four steps — the controller doesn't need a better simulator, it needs a
      gain that assumes less friction than sim did.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</WorkedExample>

<Summary>
  $$
  e_T = e_0 \,\bigl|1 - f\,K\bigr|^{\,T}
  $$

  The reality gap here isn't a bug in the physics engine — it's the simulator's clean assumption ($f=1$)
  simply not matching a real actuator's honest ($f<1$) delivery. High-throughput GPU physics engines make
  it cheap to train against thousands of randomized $f$ values instead of one, which is exactly what
  domain randomization buys: a policy whose gain was never allowed to overfit to a friction that only
  existed in simulation. The next chapters turn away from robotics and toward the infrastructure that
  serves any trained model at scale, starting with the memory hierarchy every GPU kernel has to respect.
</Summary>
