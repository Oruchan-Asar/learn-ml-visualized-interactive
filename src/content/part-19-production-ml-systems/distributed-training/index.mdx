<Hook>
  Split a batch across machines, have each one compute its own gradient, then combine them — and the update
  that comes out should be exactly the one a single machine would have computed on the whole batch. Whether
  that's actually true depends entirely on how "combine" is defined.
</Hook>

<Intuition>
  <IntuitionDemo />

  Same six numbers, split two ways. Toggle between them and watch when the naive combination — just
  averaging the workers' averages — silently stops matching the single-machine answer.
</Intuition>

<Formalize>
  A batch average is the sum of everything divided by the count of everything:

  $$
  \bar{g} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} g_i
  $$

  <Variables>
    <Variable>**$\bar{g}$** — the batch-average gradient, the value a single machine would compute over the whole batch.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$n$** — the total number of examples in the batch.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$g_i$** — the gradient computed for example $i$.</Variable>
  </Variables>

  Split into $K$ shards of sizes $n_1, \dots, n_K$, each worker computes its own shard average $\bar{g}_k$.
  Recombining them **correctly** means weighting by how many examples each worker actually saw:

  $$
  \bar{g} = \frac{\sum_k n_k \bar{g}_k}{\sum_k n_k}
  $$

  <Variables>
    <Variable>**$K$** — the number of shards (workers) the batch is split across.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$n_k$** — the number of examples shard $k$ actually received.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$\bar{g}_k$** — worker $k$'s own average gradient, computed only over its shard.</Variable>
  </Variables>

  When every shard is the same size, the weights are all equal and this collapses to a plain average of the
  workers' averages — which is exactly why the naive version looks correct until the shards stop matching in
  size.
</Formalize>

<Play>
  <PlayDemo />

  Equal shards: naive and weighted agree, because with identical $n_k$ the weighting cancels out. Unequal
  shards: naive quietly drifts to the wrong number, while weighted keeps matching the single-machine batch
  average — the same equation the whole time, just correctly applied.
</Play>

<WorkedExample>
  <Steps>
    <Step label="Six gradients, one shard of 2 and one shard of 4">
      Shard sizes 2 and 4: averages 1 and 2.5. Naive average: $(1 + 2.5)/2 = 1.75$.
    </Step>
    <Step label="The single machine's answer">
      All six gradients — $4, -2, 6, 0, -8, 12$ — sum to 12, divided by 6 is exactly $2$. Naive averaging
      missed it by 0.25, because the 4-example shard's average deserved twice the weight of the 2-example
      shard's.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Weighting by shard size fixes it">
      $\dfrac{1(2) + 2.5(4)}{2 + 4} = \dfrac{2 + 10}{6} = 2$ — exactly the single-machine value, regardless
      of how unevenly the batch was split.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</WorkedExample>

<Summary>
  $$
  \bar{g} = \frac{\sum_k n_k \bar{g}_k}{\sum_k n_k}
  $$

  Data-parallel training only produces the "same update, just faster" promise when the combination step
  actually implements this weighted average — which is exactly what a correct all-reduce does under the
  hood. Get the weighting wrong (uneven shards, a straggler worker silently dropped, a partial batch on the
  last step) and distributed training isn't an accelerated version of single-machine training anymore; it's
  a different, uncontrolled algorithm that happens to look similar. The next chapter turns to what a trained
  model does with a query at serving time: searching a huge index of vectors for the nearest few, without
  comparing against every single one.
</Summary>
