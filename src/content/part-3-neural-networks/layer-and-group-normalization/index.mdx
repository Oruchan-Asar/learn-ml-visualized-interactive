<Hook>
  Batch normalization needs a batch — it measures a mean and variance *across examples*. What happens the moment
  your batch has one example in it, or your model processes one sequence position at a time?
</Hook>

<Intuition>
  <IntuitionDemo />

  Same idea as batch norm — force a healthy mean and variance — but measured across a completely different axis:
  not across examples in a batch, but across this one example's own channels. No batch required, no matter how many
  examples you're processing at once.
</Intuition>

<Formalize>
  Split a layer's output channels into groups, then normalize each group independently using only that group's own
  statistics:

  $$
  \hat h_i = \frac{h_i - \mu_g}{\sqrt{\sigma_g^2 + \epsilon}}, \quad i \in \text{group } g
  $$

  <Variables>
    <Variable>**$h_i$** — channel $i$'s raw output, for this one example.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$\mu_g$**, **$\sigma_g^2$** — the mean and variance of *only the channels in group $g$*, for this one
    example.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$\hat h_i$** — channel $i$ after normalization.</Variable>
  </Variables>

  <Steps>
    <Step label="One group (LayerNorm)">
      Every channel lands in the same group — $\mu_g$ and $\sigma_g^2$ are the mean and variance across *all* of
      this example's channels at once.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Several groups (GroupNorm)">
      Channels split into smaller groups, each normalized against only its own members — a middle ground between
      one giant group and every channel alone.
    </Step>
    <Step label="One channel per group (InstanceNorm, the degenerate limit)">
      Each group has exactly one member, so that member's "mean" is itself — every value normalizes to exactly $0$,
      which is why this extreme is rarely useful on its own.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</Formalize>

<Play>
  <PlayDemo />

  Slide from one group up to six. The channels get shuffled into smaller and smaller clusters, each computing its
  *own* mean and variance — completely independent of whatever else is in the batch, or whether there's a batch at
  all.
</Play>

<WorkedExample>
  <Steps>
    <Step label="Six raw activations">
      $[2, -1, 0.5, 3, -2.5, 1]$ — mean $0.5$, variance $20/6\approx3.33$.
    </Step>
    <Step label="One group (LayerNorm)">
      Normalizing across all six at once gives mean $\approx0$ and variance $\approx1$ for the whole vector — the
      same guarantee batch norm gives, just measured across channels instead of across a batch.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Three groups of two">
      Each pair happens to split into two values equally far from their own mean — so *every* one of the six
      outputs lands at exactly $+1$ or $-1$, regardless of how spread out the original six numbers were.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</WorkedExample>

<Summary>
  $$
  \hat h_i = \frac{h_i - \mu_g}{\sqrt{\sigma_g^2 + \epsilon}}, \quad i \in \text{group } g
  $$

  Batch norm normalizes across examples; layer and group norm normalize across channels, for one example at a time.
  That single change in axis is what makes them usable with a batch size of 1, inside a recurrent step, or across
  variable-length sequences — exactly the settings where batch norm's batch-level statistics either don't exist or
  don't mean anything stable. The next chapter takes this same idea and strips it down even further, dropping the
  mean-centering step entirely.
</Summary>
