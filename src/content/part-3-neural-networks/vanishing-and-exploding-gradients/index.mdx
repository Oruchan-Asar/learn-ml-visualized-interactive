<Hook>
  Chapter 8 asked whether the forward pass survives depth. Backprop has to travel the exact same distance in reverse — does the gradient survive the trip?
</Hook>

<Intuition>
  <IntuitionDemo />

  This is a chain of sigmoid layers, same weight repeated at every layer. Drag depth up and watch the gradient reaching the input drop — not by a little each layer, but by roughly one more order of magnitude every single time.
</Intuition>

<Formalize>
  Going backward one layer multiplies the gradient by $\sigma'(z)\cdot w$ — Chapter 5's chain rule, once per layer. Sigmoid's derivative peaks at exactly $0.25$ and is smaller everywhere else:

  $$
  \frac{\partial L}{\partial h_{i-1}} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial h_i}\cdot \sigma'(z_i)\cdot w
  $$

  <Variables>
    <Variable>**$L$** — the loss.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$h_i$** and **$h_{i-1}$** — the hidden layer outputs at layer $i$ and the layer just before it.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$z_i$** — layer $i$'s raw weighted sum, before its activation.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$\sigma'(z_i)$** — sigmoid's derivative at that point, capped at $0.25$.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$w$** — the weight, repeated at every layer in this example.</Variable>
  </Variables>

  <Steps>
    <Step label="Repeated shrinking compounds to nothing">
      Multiply a number smaller than $1$ by itself ten times and it's gone.
    </Step>
    <Step label="No weight scale rescues sigmoid">
      Unlike Chapter 8's forward pass, there's no weight scale that fixes this — a bigger $w$ pushes $z$ further
      from $0$, which makes $\sigma'(z)$ *even smaller*, not larger. Sigmoid's derivative is capped at $0.25$, so
      this particular chain can only ever shrink going backward.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Exploding gradients are the mirror image">
      The mirror-image failure — *exploding* gradients — shows up instead in linear or ReLU layers with large
      weights, where nothing caps that per-layer factor above $1$; it's the exact same backward multiplication
      as Chapter 8's forward one, just run in reverse.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</Formalize>

<Play>
  <PlayDemo />

  Slide from a shallow to a deep chain. Five layers in, the gradient is already down around $10^{-5}$; by ten layers it's down to about $2\times10^{-9}$ — utterly unusable for updating a weight that far back. This is the **vanishing gradient problem**, and it's exactly why sigmoid fell out of favor as networks got deep.
</Play>

<WorkedExample>
  With weight $w=3$:

  <Steps>
    <Step label="Check a single layer">
      $\sigma'(z_0)\cdot w$ is at most $0.25\times3=0.75$, so even the very first backward step shrinks the
      gradient.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Follow it to depth 6">
      The gradient reaching the input is about $3.6\times10^{-6}$ — already roughly four orders of magnitude
      smaller than the loss gradient at the output, $\approx0.056$.
    </Step>
    <Step label="One layer further">
      It drops under $10^{-6}$ entirely: for practical purposes, that weight has stopped learning.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</WorkedExample>

<VanishingCheckpoint />

<Summary>
  $$
  \frac{\partial L}{\partial h_{i-1}} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial h_i}\cdot \sigma'(z_i)\cdot w
  $$

  A gradient that vanishes on the way back means the earliest layers of a deep network barely update at all — they're effectively frozen at their initial (random) values while only the last few layers learn anything. **ReLU** (short for **Rectified Linear Unit**)'s derivative doesn't have this ceiling (it's exactly $1$ wherever the neuron is active), which is a large part of why it replaced sigmoid as the default choice once networks got deep.
</Summary>
