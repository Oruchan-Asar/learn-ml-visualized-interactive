<Hook>
  Part I's chain rule found the slope of one function composed with another. A network is one neuron feeding the next feeding the next — so isn't that just the chain rule, several times over?
</Hook>

<Intuition>
  <IntuitionDemo />

  This whole curve is $y(x) = \sigma(w_2\, h(x) + b_2)$, where $h(x) = \sigma(w_1 x + b_1)$ is itself the output of a first neuron. Drag along it — the tangent line you see is the composed function's real slope, and it's built from nothing but two ordinary sigmoid derivatives.
</Intuition>

<Formalize>
  Exactly Part I's chain rule, applied once per layer:

  $$
  \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy}{dh} \cdot \frac{dh}{dx}
  $$

  <Variables>
    <Variable>**$x$** — the network's input.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$h$** — the first neuron's output, which feeds into the second neuron as its input.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$y$** — the second neuron's output, the whole composed function's final value.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$\frac{dh}{dx}$** — the first neuron's local slope: its own sigmoid derivative times $w_1$.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$\frac{dy}{dh}$** — the second neuron's local slope with respect to its input $h$: its own sigmoid derivative times $w_2$.</Variable>
  </Variables>

  $\frac{dh}{dx}$ is the first neuron's local slope — its own sigmoid derivative times $w_1$. $\frac{dy}{dh}$ is the second neuron's local slope with respect to *its* input, which happens to be $h$ — its sigmoid derivative times $w_2$. Multiply the two and you have the slope of the whole chain, with respect to the very first input.
</Formalize>

<Play>
  <PlayDemo />

  The readout splits the product into its two factors. Neither factor alone is the answer — you need to multiply straight through the chain, exactly the way Part I's chain rule always worked, just with the first function's output value substituted into the second function's slope.
</Play>

<WorkedExample>
  At $x=0.5$:

  <Steps>
    <Step label="Compute the first neuron's output and slope">
      $w_1 x + b_1 = 2(0.5)-1 = 0$, so $h = \sigma(0) = 0.5$ and
      $\frac{dh}{dx} = \sigma'(0)\cdot w_1 = 0.25 \cdot 2 = 0.5$.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Compute the second neuron's slope, at h">
      $w_2 h + b_2 = -3(0.5)+1 = -0.5$, so $\frac{dy}{dh} = \sigma'(-0.5)\cdot w_2 \approx 0.235 \cdot (-3) = -0.705$.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Multiply the two slopes together">
      $$
      \frac{dy}{dx} = -0.705 \times 0.5 = -0.353
      $$

      Matching the tangent line exactly at that point, and matching a plain numerical derivative to five decimal
      places.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</WorkedExample>

<ChainCheckpoint />

<Summary>
  $$
  \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy}{dh} \cdot \frac{dh}{dx}
  $$

  A two-layer network's gradient is a two-term product; an $n$-layer network's gradient is an $n$-term product — the exact same chain rule, repeated once per layer. That repeated multiplication, computed efficiently backward through the whole network, is what the next two chapters actually build.
</Summary>
