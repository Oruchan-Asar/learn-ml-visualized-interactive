<Intuition>

<Formalize>
  Batch normalization re-centers and rescales a layer's output *across a batch of examples*, at every layer, regardless of what produced it:

  $$
  \hat h_j = \frac{h_j - \mu_j}{\sqrt{\sigma_j^2 + \epsilon}}
  $$

  <Variables>
    <Variable>**$\hat h_j$** — neuron $j$'s output after normalization, mean $0$ and variance $1$ by construction.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$h_j$** — neuron $j$'s raw output, before normalization.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$\mu_j$** — the mean of neuron $j$'s output, measured across the current batch.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$\sigma_j^2$** — the variance of neuron $j$'s output, measured across the current batch.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$\epsilon$** — a tiny constant that keeps the division from blowing up when the variance is near zero.</Variable>
  </Variables>

  $\mu_j$ and $\sigma_j^2$ are the mean and variance of neuron $j$'s output, measured across the current batch. After this, every neuron has mean $0$ and variance $1$ — by construction, not by hoping the weight scale happened to be right.
</Formalize>

<Play>
  <PlayDemo />

  Drag the scale anywhere — tiny, huge, exactly ideal. The faint line swings across nine orders of magnitude. The bold one barely leaves $1$. Batch norm doesn't care what scale produced the input; it forces the *output* to a fixed scale every time.
</Play>

<WorkedExample>
  <Steps>
    <Step label="Without batch norm, at scale 0.05">
      Six layers shrink the **RMS** (short for **Root Mean Square**) activation from about $1$ down to under $10^{-5}$ — dead, exactly as in Chapter 8.
    </Step>
    <Step label="With batch norm, at every scale tried">
      Insert batch normalization after each layer instead, and every single layer's RMS activation comes out
      within about $0.5\%$ of exactly $1$ — from $0.05$ all the way to $1.0$.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Compare the spread">
      The five-scale spread in final-layer RMS with batch norm is under $0.05$, compared to nine orders of
      magnitude without it.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</WorkedExample>

<BatchNormCheckpoint />

<Summary>
  $$
  \hat h_j = \frac{h_j - \mu_j}{\sqrt{\sigma_j^2 + \epsilon}}
  $$

  Batch norm doesn't fix a bad weight scale — it makes the weight scale stop mattering, by re-establishing a healthy activation distribution at every layer regardless of what arrived there. That's also why training got noticeably more forgiving of learning rate and initialization choices once batch norm became standard. The next chapter looks at a completely different kind of fix — one aimed not at unstable activations, but at a network that's learned its training data a little too well.
</Summary>
