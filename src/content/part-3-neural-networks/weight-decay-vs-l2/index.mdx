<Hook>
  Two textbooks tell you "L2 regularization" and "weight decay" are the same thing. With plain SGD, they're right. Plug either one into Adam, and they quietly stop agreeing.
</Hook>

<Intuition>
  <IntuitionDemo />

  Two weights, $w_1$ and $w_2$, start identical. The only difference is their optimizer history: $w_1$ has barely been
  updated (small accumulated squared gradient $v$), $w_2$ has been updated constantly (large $v$). Watch what the
  *same* $\lambda$ does to each under the two decay schemes.
</Intuition>

<Formalize>
  L2 regularization is usually implemented by adding $\lambda w$ to the gradient before the optimizer's update rule
  divides by $\sqrt{v}+\epsilon$. Weight decay (as in AdamW) skips that division entirely:

  $$
  \Delta w_{\text{L2}} = \frac{\eta\,\lambda\,w}{\sqrt{v}+\epsilon} \qquad \Delta w_{\text{decoupled}} = \eta\,\lambda\,w
  $$

  <Variables>
    <Variable>**$\eta$** — the learning rate.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$\lambda$** — the regularization strength.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$w$** — the current weight value.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$v$** — Adam's accumulated squared-gradient history for this parameter.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$\epsilon$** — a tiny constant that keeps the division well-defined.</Variable>
  </Variables>

  <Steps>
    <Step label="Plain SGD: v is effectively 1 for every parameter">
      SGD has no per-parameter adaptive scaling, so both formulas reduce to the same thing: $\Delta w = \eta\lambda w$.
      There's no distinction to make — this is why the two terms got used interchangeably for years.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Adam: v varies wildly per parameter">
      A parameter with a small $v$ (rarely updated) gets *divided by a small number*, so $\Delta w_{\text{L2}}$ comes
      out close to $\eta\lambda w$ — full-strength regularization. A parameter with a large $v$ (frequently updated)
      gets divided by a large number, so its coupled decay shrinks toward zero no matter how large $\lambda$ is.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Decoupled decay never looks at v">
      $\Delta w_{\text{decoupled}} = \eta\lambda w$ doesn't reference $v$ at all — every parameter decays by the same
      fraction of itself, exactly matching the original intent of "shrink every weight a little."
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</Formalize>

<Play>
  <PlayDemo />

  Push $\lambda$ up. The two coupled bars (L2-in-Adam) spread apart — $w_1$'s decay grows fast, $w_2$'s barely moves.
  The two decoupled bars stay stacked on top of each other the entire time, because decoupled decay was never looking
  at each parameter's gradient history in the first place.
</Play>

<WorkedExample>
  <Steps>
    <Step label="Same λ = 0.5, two parameters">
      $w_1$ has $v=0.01$ (so $\sqrt{v}=0.1$), $w_2$ has $v=4$ (so $\sqrt{v}=2$). With $\eta=0.1$: coupled decay on
      $w_1$ is $\approx 0.1\times0.5\times1 / 0.1 = 0.5$ — half the weight, in one step. Coupled decay on $w_2$ is
      $0.1\times0.5\times1/2=0.025$ — twenty times smaller, for the identical $\lambda$.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Decoupled decay doesn't care">
      At that same $\lambda=0.5$, decoupled decay gives *both* parameters exactly $0.1\times0.5\times1=0.05$ — a
      fixed 5% shrink, independent of how often either parameter has been updated.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Why this matters in practice">
      Rarely-updated parameters (think: embeddings for infrequent tokens) get crushed by coupled L2 long before
      frequently-updated ones feel any pull — an accident of optimizer bookkeeping, not a deliberate regularization
      choice. Decoupled decay applies the *same* regularization strength everywhere, which is why AdamW dropped
      coupled L2 in favor of it.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</WorkedExample>

<Summary>
  $$
  \Delta w_{\text{L2}} = \frac{\eta\,\lambda\,w}{\sqrt{v}+\epsilon} \qquad \Delta w_{\text{decoupled}} = \eta\,\lambda\,w
  $$

  "Weight decay" and "L2 regularization" are the same formula only when there's no adaptive per-parameter scaling to
  interact with. The moment you add one — Adam, RMSProp, anything that divides by an accumulated gradient
  statistic — coupling the two lets the optimizer's own bookkeeping decide how much regularization each parameter
  actually receives. Decoupling them, as AdamW does, restores $\lambda$ to meaning what it says. The next few
  chapters look at other places training goes wrong not because the math is broken, but because a hyperparameter
  interacts with something it was never designed to interact with — starting with the learning rate itself.
</Summary>
