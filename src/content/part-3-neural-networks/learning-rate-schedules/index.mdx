<Hook>
  A learning rate that's perfect once training has settled down is often wildly too large at the very first step,
  while everything is still far from where it needs to be. One fixed number can't be right for both moments.
</Hook>

<Intuition>
  <IntuitionDemo />

  This toy problem just wants to minimize $f(x)=x^2$ starting from $x=10$. The base rate here is deliberately too
  large to use constantly. Watch $\log_{10}(\text{loss})$ over 12 steps — it doesn't wobble, it climbs, forever.
</Intuition>

<Formalize>
  Two schedules address this from different angles. Step decay reduces the rate at fixed checkpoints:

  $$
  \eta_{\text{step}}(t) = \eta_0 \cdot \gamma^{\lfloor t / k \rfloor}
  $$

  Warmup + cosine instead starts the rate at (near) zero and ramps it up before easing it back down:

  $$
  \eta_{\text{warmup}}(t) = \begin{cases} \eta_0 \cdot \dfrac{t}{T_w} & t < T_w \\[4pt] \dfrac{\eta_0}{2}\left(1+\cos\!\left(\pi \cdot \dfrac{t - T_w}{T - T_w}\right)\right) & t \geq T_w \end{cases}
  $$

  <Variables>
    <Variable>**$\eta_0$** — the base (peak) learning rate.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$\gamma$** — the step-decay shrink factor applied every $k$ steps.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$T_w$** — the number of warmup steps, ramping linearly from $0$ up to $\eta_0$.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$T$** — the total number of training steps, by which the cosine term reaches its floor.</Variable>
  </Variables>

  <Steps>
    <Step label="Step decay still starts at the full (too-large) rate">
      Until the first checkpoint at $t=k$, $\eta_{\text{step}}(t)=\eta_0$ — exactly the same too-large rate as never
      decaying at all. Whatever damage a too-large rate does in those first $k$ steps still happens.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Warmup never applies the full rate until x has already moved toward the optimum">
      $\eta_{\text{warmup}}(0)=0$, and it only reaches $\eta_0$ at $t=T_w$ — by which point gradient descent has
      already taken several small, safe steps. The large rate arrives once it's less likely to cause an overshoot.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</Formalize>

<Play>
  <PlayDemo />

  Switch between all three. Step decay eventually gets the loss down too — but only after a huge overshoot in the
  middle, while it's still using the full base rate. Warmup + cosine reaches a comparably tiny final loss without
  ever letting the loss climb above where it started.
</Play>

<WorkedExample>
  <Steps>
    <Step label="Constant: diverges immediately and never recovers">
      At the base rate, $x_{t+1}=x_t(1-2\eta_0)=x_t\cdot(-1.4)$ — each step *grows* in magnitude by 40%.
      Since every step multiplies by the same $-1.4$, this is a plain geometric sequence:
      $x_t = 10\cdot(-1.4)^t$. At $t=12$: $x_{12}=10\cdot(-1.4)^{12}\approx566.9$, and the loss
      $x_{12}^2\approx321{,}000$ — up from $100$ at the start.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Step decay: the same explosion, temporarily">
      For the first 4 steps (before the first decay), step decay is identical to constant, so the same
      closed form applies: $x_4 = 10\cdot(-1.4)^4 \approx 38.4$, and loss $x_4^2\approx1476$ — nearly
      $15\times$ its starting value, before the rate ever drops. Once it does, the trajectory recovers
      quickly and the final loss ends up tiny — but only after that mid-run spike.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Warmup + cosine: no spike at all">
      During warmup, $\eta(t)=1.2\cdot t/4$ is a different rate every step, so each step needs its own
      multiplication:
      - $t=0$: $\eta=0$, so $x_1=10\cdot(1-0)=10$ — completely unchanged
      - $t=1$: $\eta=0.3$, so $x_2=10\cdot(1-0.6)=4$
      - $t=2$: $\eta=0.6$, so $x_3=4\cdot(1-1.2)=-0.8$
      - $t=3$: $\eta=0.9$, so $x_4=-0.8\cdot(1-1.8)=0.64$

      By the time the rate ramps up to its peak at $t=4$, $x$ has already shrunk to $0.64$ on its own from
      those small early steps. The loss never exceeds its starting value of $100$ anywhere along the run,
      and still ends near $6\times10^{-4}$.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</WorkedExample>

<Summary>
  $$
  \eta_{\text{warmup}}(t) = \begin{cases} \eta_0 \cdot t/T_w & t < T_w \\ \frac{\eta_0}{2}\left(1+\cos\left(\pi\frac{t-T_w}{T-T_w}\right)\right) & t \geq T_w \end{cases}
  $$

  A constant learning rate has to compromise between "large enough to make progress" and "small enough not to
  overshoot" for every step of training at once — usually failing at both once the ideal rate changes over time.
  Step decay fixes the long run but not the start. Warmup fixes the start; cosine decay fixes the end. Together
  they let the rate be small when the network can't yet handle anything larger, large once it can, and small again
  for fine convergence — which is exactly why a flat rate is the exception in modern training, not the rule. The
  next chapter turns to a different training-time constraint: numeric precision itself.
</Summary>
