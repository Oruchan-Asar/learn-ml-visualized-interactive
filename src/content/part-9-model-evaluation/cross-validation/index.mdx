<Hook>
  Every model in this course so far has been scored by fitting on some data and testing on the rest. But *which* points end up in the test set is a choice — and a different choice can tell a completely different story about how good the model is.
</Hook>

<Intuition>
  <IntuitionDemo />

  Ten points trend almost perfectly along a line — except one, which sits far off it. Step through five different train/test splits of the same ten points and watch the held-out error swing from barely-there to enormous, depending only on whether that one point happened to land in the test set.
</Intuition>

<Formalize>
  **k-fold cross-validation** partitions the data into $k$ equal folds. For each fold in turn, train on the other $k-1$ folds and evaluate on the one held out:

  $$
  \text{CV error} = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k} \text{MSE}(\text{model trained without fold } i,\ \text{tested on fold } i)
  $$

  <Variables>
    <Variable>**CV error** — the k-fold cross-validation estimate of error: the average held-out error across every fold.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$k$** — the number of folds the data is split into.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$i$** — the index of the current fold, ranging from $1$ to $k$.</Variable>
    <Variable>**MSE** — mean squared error: the metric used to score each fold's held-out predictions.</Variable>
  </Variables>

  <Steps>
    <Step label="A single split is one sample from the process">
      A single train/test split gives you one sample from this process — one particular way the anomaly (or any
      unusual point) could land, for better or worse.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Repeating it reveals disagreement, not just a mean">
      Cross-validation runs the experiment $k$ times and reports both the mean *and* how much the folds
      disagreed — a spread that a single split can never reveal about itself.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</Formalize>

<Play>
  <PlayDemo />

  Four of the five folds score a modest error. The one fold holding out the anomaly alone scores dramatically worse — over 30 times higher than the best fold. The mean folds all five outcomes into a single honest number, and the stdev quantifies exactly how much that number should be trusted.
</Play>

<WorkedExample>
  Ten points following $y = 2x + 1$, except $x=6$, where $y=30$ instead of the line's $13$:

  <Steps>
    <Step label="Most folds barely notice the anomaly">
      Holding out $x=3,4$ (fit on the other 8, including the anomaly) gives MSE $\approx 4.52$ — the anomaly pulls
      the fitted line slightly, but the held-out points still land close to it.
    </Step>
    <Step label="One fold holds out the anomaly itself">
      Holding out $x=5,6$ trains on 8 clean points, recovering the line almost exactly ($y = 2x+1$). But testing on
      $x=6$ where the true value is $30$ against a prediction of $13$ costs a squared error of $17^2=289$ — MSE $=144.5$.
    </Step>
    <Step label="One number, or five, or the truth">
      Averaging all five folds gives mean MSE $\approx 38.60$ with stdev $\approx 59.83$ — a spread almost as large as the
      mean itself. A single 80/20 split (train on the first 8, test on the last 2) happens to score $26.73$: not
      wrong, but silent about how much luckier or unluckier it could have been.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</WorkedExample>

<CrossValidationCheckpoint />

<Summary>
  $$
  \text{CV error} = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\text{MSE}_i, \qquad \text{stdev across folds tells you how much to trust it}
  $$

  A single train/test split answers "how did the model do on this particular split?" k-fold cross-validation answers the question that actually matters: "how did the model do, and how much would that answer have changed if the data had been split differently?" Every metric from the last two chapters — accuracy, F1, AUC — inherits this same fragility, and everything in this chapter applies to them exactly the same way.
</Summary>
