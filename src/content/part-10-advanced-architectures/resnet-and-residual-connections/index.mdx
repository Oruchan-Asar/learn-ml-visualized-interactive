<Hook>
  Part III showed a plain deep network's gradient shrinking toward zero, one sigmoid layer at a time. Adding more layers should make a network more powerful — instead, past a certain depth, it makes it untrainable. What if each layer didn't have to replace its input, only adjust it?
</Hook>

<Intuition>
  <IntuitionDemo />

  Same depth, same per-layer weight, same nonlinearity — the only difference is whether each layer computes a brand new value or adds a small adjustment on top of the one it received. Step through the depths and watch one gradient collapse while the other holds steady.
</Intuition>

<Formalize>
  A plain layer replaces its input entirely: $h_i = F(h_{i-1})$. A **residual (skip) connection** instead adds the layer's output back onto its input:

  $$
  h_i = h_{i-1} + F(h_{i-1})
  $$

  Backpropagating through a plain layer multiplies the gradient by $F'(h_{i-1})$ — a term that's often well below 1, so a long chain of layers shrinks it toward zero. Backpropagating through a *residual* layer multiplies by $1 + F'(h_{i-1})$ instead. That leading $1$ is a direct, unobstructed path for the gradient — an "identity shortcut" that depth alone can't erode.
</Formalize>

<Play>
  <PlayDemo />

  The plain network's gradient shrinks so fast with depth that its bars are barely visible next to the residual network's. By depth 20, the plain network's gradient has vanished to the order of $10^{-17}$; the residual network's has stayed above $100$.
</Play>

<WorkedExample>
  $F(h) = w\cdot\sigma(wh)$ with $w=3$, starting from $h_0=0.5$, at depths 5, 10, and 20:

  <Steps>
    <Step label="The plain network vanishes on schedule">
      $|\partial \mathcal{L}/\partial h_0| \approx 2.29\times10^{-5}$ at depth 5, $2.21\times10^{-9}$ at depth 10,
      and $2.06\times10^{-17}$ at depth 20 — each additional 5 layers costs roughly 4 more orders of magnitude,
      exactly the compounding-small-derivatives story from Part III.
    </Step>
    <Step label="The residual network doesn't just survive — it stabilizes">
      The same computation with a skip connection gives $\approx 32.7$ at depth 5, $\approx 67.9$ at depth 10, and
      $\approx 138.3$ at depth 20. Once the block saturates, each additional layer multiplies the gradient by a
      factor extremely close to $1$ — layers 5 through 10 change the gradient by less than $0.001$, while the
      first two layers (before saturation) still shift it by tens.
    </Step>
    <Step label="One added term changes everything">
      Both networks use the exact same $F$. The only difference is whether $h_i$ *replaces* $h_{i-1}$ or *adds to*
      it — and that single addition is the entire fix. No new gate, no new parameter, just $+h_{i-1}$.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</WorkedExample>

<ResNetCheckpoint />

<Summary>
  $$
  h_i = h_{i-1} + F(h_{i-1}) \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{\partial h_i}{\partial h_{i-1}} = 1 + F'(h_{i-1}) \geq 1 \text{ (near saturation)}
  $$

  This is the same fix the GRU and LSTM chapters made across *time* — a $1+$ term guaranteeing the gradient has an unobstructed path — now applied across *depth* instead. Wherever a network needs to stack many transformations without each one eroding what came before, the same identity-shortcut idea reappears. The next chapter turns from stacking more layers to a completely different way of representing data: compressing it down to a small latent space and learning to generate new examples from it.
</Summary>
