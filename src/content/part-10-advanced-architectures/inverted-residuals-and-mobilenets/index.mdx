<Hook>
  A ResNet block runs a full dense convolution at every layer — every output channel looking at every input channel, at every position in the kernel. That's exactly the kind of arithmetic a phone's battery can't afford. What if most of those multiplications were spent re-mixing channels that filtering and mixing didn't need to happen together at all?
</Hook>

<Intuition>
  <IntuitionDemo />

  Same 3x3 kernel, same 4x4 output, same input and output channel count — the only difference is whether one dense convolution does the whole job, or two much smaller ones split it in half. Step through the channel counts and watch the gap between the two bars grow.
</Intuition>

<Formalize>
  A standard $K\times K$ convolution mixes space and channels in a single operation. **Depthwise separable convolution** splits that into two cheaper steps: a **depthwise** conv that filters each channel on its own (space only), followed by a **pointwise** $1\times1$ conv that mixes channels (no spatial extent at all):

  $$
  \text{separable params} = \underbrace{K^2 C}_{\text{depthwise}} + \underbrace{C^2}_{\text{pointwise}} \qquad\text{vs.}\qquad \text{standard params} = K^2 C^2
  $$

  <Variables>
    <Variable>**$K$** — the kernel's spatial size (here, $3$).</Variable>
    <Variable>**$C$** — the number of channels (input and output are equal here, so a residual add is possible).</Variable>
  </Variables>

  <Steps>
    <Step label="The ratio is exact, and independent of image size">
      Dividing separable by standard params gives $\frac{K^2C + C^2}{K^2C^2} = \frac{1}{C} + \frac{1}{K^2}$ — a
      clean identity that holds at any spatial resolution, since the spatial factor cancels out entirely.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Wider layers save more, not less">
      As $C$ grows, $1/C$ shrinks toward $0$, so the ratio approaches a floor of $1/K^2 = 1/9$ — roughly a
      $9\times$ reduction at the widest layers, versus a much smaller saving at narrow ones.
    </Step>
    <Step label="MobileNet inverts the usual bottleneck order">
      An **inverted-residual** block puts this depthwise filter *inside* an expand-then-project bottleneck:
      a $1\times1$ expands $C$ channels to $tC$, a depthwise $K\times K$ filters the expanded space, and a
      linear $1\times1$ projects back down to $C$ so the block's output can add back onto its input — the
      reverse of a ResNet bottleneck, which narrows before it widens.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</Formalize>

<WorkedExample>
  $K=3$, a $4\times4$ output feature map, $C=4$ channels, expansion $t=6$:

  <Steps>
    <Step label="Standard vs. separable at 4 channels">
      - Standard: $K^2C^2 = 9\cdot16=144$ params, $144\times16=2304$ FLOPs
      - Separable, depthwise: $K^2C=9\cdot4=36$ params
      - Separable, pointwise: $C^2=16$ params
      - Separable, total: $36+16=52$ params, $52\times16=832$ FLOPs

      About $36\%$ of the standard conv's cost, matching $1/4+1/9\approx0.361$ exactly.
    </Step>
    <Step label="The inverted-residual bottleneck, expanded to 24 channels">
      - Expand $4\to24$: $4\times24=96$ params
      - Depthwise $3\times3$ on $24$ channels: $9\times24=216$ params
      - Project $24\to4$: $24\times4=96$ params

      Total: $96+216+96=408$ params.
    </Step>
    <Step label="What a dense middle conv would have cost instead">
      Swap the depthwise filter for a *dense* $3\times3$ over the same expanded $24$ channels:
      $9\times24\times24=5184$ params, plus the same two $1\times1$ layers, totals $5376$ — over
      $13\times$ the inverted-residual block's $408$. The entire saving lives in that one swapped layer.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</WorkedExample>

<Summary>
  $$
  \frac{\text{separable params}}{\text{standard params}} = \frac{1}{C} + \frac{1}{K^2}
  $$

  Splitting spatial filtering from channel mixing turns one expensive dense convolution into two cheap ones, saving more as layers get wider. MobileNet's inverted-residual block puts that same depthwise trick inside an expand-then-project bottleneck — the mirror image of a ResNet block's narrow-then-wide shape — so a network can keep the residual connection's training benefits while running on a fraction of the compute. The next chapter picks up a pretrained network like this one and asks how much of it can be reused as-is.
</Summary>
