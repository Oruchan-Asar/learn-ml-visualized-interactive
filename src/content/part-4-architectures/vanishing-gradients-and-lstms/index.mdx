<Hook>
  Chapter 6 ended with a warning: pushing a recurrent weight hard enough to get a confident answer, repeated over many timesteps, is exactly the vanishing-gradient problem from Part III — just running through time instead of through layers.
</Hook>

<Intuition>
  <IntuitionDemo />

  An **RNN (Recurrent Neural Network)** unrolled over $T$ timesteps is a $T$-layer chain with the *same* weight reused at every layer — Part III's vanishing-gradient math, applied to time. Slide the timestep count and watch the plain RNN's gradient (dim line) collapse by roughly an order of magnitude every step, while an **LSTM (Long Short-Term Memory)**'s cell-state gradient (bold line) barely bends.
</Intuition>

<Formalize>
  A plain RNN's gradient reaching timestep 1 from timestep $T$ is a product of $T{-}1$ factors, each one a squashing derivative times the recurrent weight:

  $$
  \frac{\partial h_T}{\partial h_1} = \prod_{t=2}^{T} \tanh'(z_t)\, W_h
  $$

  <Variables>
    <Variable>**$\dfrac{\partial h_T}{\partial h_1}$** — the gradient of the last hidden state with respect to the first: how much a change at step 1 still affects step $T$.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$\tanh'(z_t)$** — the derivative of the tanh activation at timestep $t$'s pre-activation, a value always less than $1$ that shrinks the gradient at every step.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$W_h$** — the recurrent weight reused at every timestep.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$T, t$** — the total number of timesteps, and $t$ the index running from $2$ up to $T$.</Variable>
  </Variables>

  An LSTM keeps a separate **cell state** $c_t = f_t \, c_{t-1} + i_t \, g_t$, and its backward path through the cell state is just:

  $$
  \frac{\partial c_T}{\partial c_1} = \prod_{t=2}^{T} f_t
  $$

  <Variables>
    <Variable>**$\dfrac{\partial c_T}{\partial c_1}$** — the gradient of the final cell state with respect to the first cell state.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$c_t$** — the LSTM's cell state at timestep $t$: a separate running memory, distinct from the hidden state.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$f_t$** — the forget gate at timestep $t$: how much of the previous cell state to keep, between $0$ and $1$.</Variable>
  </Variables>

  <Steps>
    <Step label="No squashing-derivative factor">
      Unlike the plain RNN's gradient, there's no $\tanh'$ factor in this product — nothing here is forced below $1$ by a squashing derivative at every step.
    </Step>
    <Step label="A forget gate near 1 preserves the gradient">
      If the forget gate $f_t$ stays close to $1$, this product barely shrinks at all — the "constant error carousel" that gives LSTMs their long memory.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</Formalize>

<Play>
  <PlayDemo />

  Both curves use the *exact same weight value* — this isn't about the LSTM having bigger numbers to work with. The plain RNN multiplies by a squashing derivative *and* the weight at every step; the LSTM's cell-state path multiplies by only the forget gate. Removing one shrinking factor per timestep is the entire difference, and over enough timesteps it compounds into an astronomical gap.
</Play>

<WorkedExample>
  At weight (or forget gate) $0.9$ and $10$ timesteps:

  <Steps>
    <Step label="Plain RNN gradient">
      About $2.9\times10^{-10}$ — nine orders of magnitude gone.
    </Step>
    <Step label="LSTM cell-state gradient">
      $0.9^9 \approx 0.387$ — still very much alive.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Compare">
      The ratio between them is over a billion, using the *same* $0.9$ on both sides.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</WorkedExample>

<Summary>
  $$
  \frac{\partial c_T}{\partial c_1} = \prod_{t=2}^{T} f_t \qquad \text{vs.} \qquad \frac{\partial h_T}{\partial h_1} = \prod_{t=2}^{T} \tanh'(z_t)\, W_h
  $$

  Gating doesn't eliminate the product-of-many-factors structure that causes vanishing gradients — it just removes the squashing derivative from one of the paths, so that path can carry a signal across far more timesteps before it's gone. Everything built so far — convolution, pooling, recurrence, gating — has operated on numeric vectors. The next few chapters turn to how text becomes those vectors in the first place.
</Summary>
