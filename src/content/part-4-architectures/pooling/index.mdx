<Hook>
  Convolution kept the image's spatial size almost intact — a 6×6 image gave a 4×4 feature map. Stack a few more filter layers on top of that and the numbers pile up fast. Something has to shrink.
</Hook>

<Intuition>
  <IntuitionDemo />

  This feature map has two detections: a strong one (6) in the top-left window, a weaker one (4) in the bottom-right. Toggle between max and average pooling. Max pooling reports the 6 exactly — it just asks "was this feature detected anywhere in the window?" Average pooling reports 3 — it blends the strong detection with the zeros around it, and the signal gets weaker just from being averaged.
</Intuition>

<Formalize>
  Pooling slides a window across a feature map like convolution does, but instead of a learned dot product it applies a fixed reduction — usually the max or the mean — to every value in the window:

  $$
  \text{maxpool}(r,c) = \max_{i,j} \ \text{feature}(r{\cdot}s+i,\ c{\cdot}s+j)
  $$

  <Variables>
    <Variable>**$\text{maxpool}(r,c)$** — the pooled output value at row $r$, column $c$.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$\text{feature}(\cdot)$** — the input feature map produced by the convolution layer.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$s$** — the stride: how many positions the window shifts between neighboring windows.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$r, c$** — the row and column of the output (pooled) position.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$i, j$** — the row and column offsets ranging over the pooling window.</Variable>
  </Variables>

  where $s$ is the stride. A $2\times2$ window with stride $2$ turns this $4\times4$ feature map into a $2\times2$ one — a quarter of the values, chosen so nothing important gets thrown away.
</Formalize>

<Play>
  <PlayDemo />

  Move the window to the bottom-right corner. Max pooling reports $4$ — the one real detection there survives untouched. Average pooling reports $1$ — a quarter of $4$, diluted by three zeros. Now check the other two windows: both poolings agree on $0$, because there's nothing there to lose in the first place. The disagreement only shows up exactly where there's a strong, localized signal — which is exactly why max pooling is the default choice after a convolution layer.
</Play>

<WorkedExample>
  <Steps>
    <Step label="Top-left window">
      Rows 0-1 and columns 0-1 hold the values $2, 2, 2, 6$. Max pooling takes $\max(2,2,2,6) = 6$ — the exact
      strength of the strongest detection, unchanged. Average pooling takes $\frac{2+2+2+6}{4} = 3$ — half of
      the true peak, because it's been averaged against three much smaller values.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Bottom-right window">
      The values $4, 0, 0, 0$ tell the same story at a smaller scale: max reports $4$, average reports
      $\frac{4}{4}=1$.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</WorkedExample>

<PoolingCheckpoint />

<Summary>
  $$
  \text{maxpool}(r,c) = \max_{i,j} \ \text{feature}(r{\cdot}s+i,\ c{\cdot}s+j)
  $$

  Pooling shrinks a feature map by a fixed, un-learned rule, trading spatial precision (exactly where in the window something was) for a smaller, more manageable representation — while max pooling keeps the actual strength of whatever was detected. The next chapter puts a filter layer and a pooling layer together, back to back, into the first real convolutional network.
</Summary>
