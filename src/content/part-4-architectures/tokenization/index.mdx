<Hook>
  Last chapter's word vectors assumed every word already had a slot in a fixed vocabulary. Real text is unbounded — new words, typos, names, made-up words appear constantly. Something has to decide, before any vector is even looked up, what counts as "one unit."
</Hook>

<Intuition>
  <IntuitionDemo />

  Start from raw characters and merge the single most frequent adjacent pair, over and over. Drag the slider forward and watch "newest" collapse from 7 individual characters down to just 3 pieces — each merge fusing together whatever pair showed up most often across the whole training vocabulary.
</Intuition>

<Formalize>
  <Steps>
    <Step label="Byte-pair encoding, defined">
      This algorithm is **byte-pair encoding (BPE)**: starting from individual characters, repeatedly find
      the most frequent adjacent pair of symbols across the whole corpus and merge it into one new symbol,
      forming a growing vocabulary of subword pieces.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Trained on just four words">
      Trained on a corpus of just four words — low, lower, newest, widest — it discovers pieces like
      <code>est&lt;/w&gt;</code> (a common suffix) and <code>low</code> (a common stem), simply because those
      pairs of symbols kept showing up together.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</Formalize>

<Play>
  <PlayDemo />

  Type any word. These merge rules were learned from only four training words, yet they generalize: type "lowest" — a word the algorithm never saw — and it still gets cut cleanly into <code>low</code> + <code>est&lt;/w&gt;</code>, reusing pieces learned from completely different words ("low" from *low/lower*, "est&lt;/w&gt;" from *newest/widest*). That's the entire point of subword tokenization: no word is ever truly unknown, because it can always fall back to pieces — down to individual characters if it has to.
</Play>

<WorkedExample>
  <Steps>
    <Step label="Start from characters">
      Tokenizing "lowest" from scratch: $l\ o\ w\ e\ s\ t\ \langle/w\rangle$.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Apply the learned merges in order">
      1. $e{+}s\to es$
      2. $es{+}t\to est$
      3. $est{+}\langle/w\rangle \to est\langle/w\rangle$
      4. $l{+}o\to lo$
      5. $lo{+}w\to low$
    </Step>
    <Step label="Result">
      Exactly two tokens: $low$ and $est\langle/w\rangle$. Every merge rule came from *low*, *lower*,
      *newest*, and *widest* alone; "lowest" itself was never part of training.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</WorkedExample>

<Summary>
  Byte-pair encoding is exactly what **GPT** (short for **Generative Pre-trained Transformer**)-style models use in practice — trained on billions of words instead of four, producing tens of thousands of merge rules instead of six, but the algorithm is identical: start from characters, merge the most frequent pair, repeat. Every one of those pieces gets an integer ID, and *that* integer is what finally goes into the embedding lookup from the last chapter. With text now reliably turned into a sequence of IDs, the next chapters ask how a model reads that whole sequence at once — starting with attention.
</Summary>
