<Hook>
  Anyone can claim to be "the next block." With no central authority to pick a winner, how does a network
  of strangers agree on whose claim actually counts — in a way that's cheap to check but expensive to fake?
</Hook>

<Intuition>
  <IntuitionDemo />

  Slide the nonce and watch the hash bounce around with no pattern at all — there's no way to guess which
  nonce will land under the target line except by trying it. That randomness is the entire point: finding
  a winning nonce takes real, unavoidable work, but once found, anyone can verify it in one hash.
</Intuition>

<Formalize>
  <Steps>
    <Step label="A block hashes its own contents plus a nonce">
      $\text{blockHash}(\text{prevHash}, \text{data}, \text{nonce}) = \text{toyHash}(\text{prevHash} \mathbin\Vert \text{data} \mathbin\Vert \text{nonce})$
      — the same hash from the last chapter, now folding in a free variable: the **nonce**.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Proof-of-work is a threshold on that hash">
      A nonce "wins" if $\text{blockHash}(\ldots) < T$ for a **difficulty target** $T$. Lower $T$ means
      fewer winning nonces out of every 1000 possible hash values — harder to find, but the check itself
      (one hash, one comparison) stays instant either way.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Mining is brute-force search, nothing cleverer">
      There's no shortcut: try $\text{nonce}=0,1,2,\ldots$ and hash each one until $\text{blockHash} < T$.
      With hashes landing uniformly across $[0,1000)$, a fraction $T/1000$ of nonces succeed, so the
      **expected number of attempts** before the first success is $1000/T$.
    </Step>
    <Step label="This is Nakamoto consensus's actual mechanism">
      Whoever finds a winning nonce first gets to propose the next block. Because that costs real,
      unfakeable computational work, the network agrees on *whoever paid the most to get there* — no vote,
      no central authority, just a race everyone can independently verify the winner of.
    </Step>
  </Steps>

  <Variables>
    <Variable>**prevHash** — the previous block's hash, chaining this block to everything before it.</Variable>
    <Variable>**nonce** — the only value miners are free to change; searched from 0 up to 999 here.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$T$** — the difficulty target; a hash below it counts as a valid block.</Variable>
  </Variables>
</Formalize>

<Play>
  <PlayDemo />

  Step through nonces one at a time on the genesis block. Every single attempt costs the same one hash —
  there's no way to skip ahead, and no way to tell in advance how many attempts it'll take. That's the
  "cost" in proof-of-*work*: real energy spent per attempt, with no guarantee of when it pays off.
</Play>

<WorkedExample>
  Mining the genesis block: `prevHash = 0`, `data = "block-one"`, target $T=50$.

  <Steps>
    <Step label="Try the first few nonces">
      $\text{blockHash}(0,\texttt{"block-one"},0)=934$, then $935, 936, \ldots$ for nonces $1$ through $9$
      — every one of them lands in the 930s, nowhere near under $50$.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Nonce 10 finally succeeds">
      $\text{blockHash}(0,\texttt{"block-one"},10)=33$. Since $33 < 50$, this block is now mined — it took
      $11$ attempts (nonces $0$ through $10$).
    </Step>
    <Step label="Chain a second block onto it">
      With `prevHash = 33` (the genesis block's hash) and `data = "checkpoint-block"`, the first winning
      nonce is $90$, giving hash $7$ — a completely different search, because prevHash changed the entire
      input.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Compare to the expected attempt count">
      $1000/T = 1000/50 = 20$ expected attempts on average. The genesis block happened to need only $11$;
      the second needed $91$ — mining is a race against probability, not a guarantee.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</WorkedExample>

<Summary>
  $$
  \text{blockHash}(\text{prevHash}, \text{data}, \text{nonce}) < T
  $$

  Proof-of-work turns "who proposes the next block" into a race that costs real computation to win but
  costs almost nothing to verify — the asymmetry that lets a network of mutually distrusting nodes agree
  without a referee. The next chapters look at what happens when two miners find a valid block at nearly
  the same time (forks), and at an entirely different way to make block proposal expensive: staking money
  instead of burning electricity.
</Summary>
