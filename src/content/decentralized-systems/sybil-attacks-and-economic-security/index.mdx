import { IntuitionDemo, PlayDemo, AttackCostCheckpoint } from "./Demos";

<Hook>
  Nothing stops you from generating a thousand keypairs and claiming to be a thousand different nodes.
  If a network counted votes "one per identity," that would be game over. So how does a system that lets
  anyone join stay hard to take over?
</Hook>

<Intuition>
  <IntuitionDemo />

  Creating fake identities costs nothing — that bar sits at zero no matter how many you generate. The
  bars that actually cost something are the ones backed by a real, scarce resource: hash power you have
  to buy and run, or stake you have to actually own. Identity is free; influence isn't.
</Intuition>

<Formalize>
  <Steps>
    <Step label="A Sybil attack is fake identities, not fake resources">
      Generating $n$ keypairs costs $\text{sybilCost}(n) = n \times \text{costPerIdentity}$. In a naive
      peer-to-peer network, $\text{costPerIdentity} = 0$, so this is $0$ regardless of $n$ — a thousand
      "nodes" for free.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Proof-of-work and proof-of-stake price influence, not identity count">
      $\text{attackCost}(\text{share}, \text{totalValue}) = \text{share} \times \text{totalValue}$. It
      doesn't matter whether an attacker splits their acquired resource across 1 identity or 10,000 —
      the cost only depends on how much of the total resource they end up controlling.
    </Step>
    <Step label="The 51% threshold is where an attacker can start overriding the honest majority">
      Below 50% share, honest nodes' combined resource still outweighs the attacker's on every rule that
      relies on majority agreement (longest-chain, majority stake-weighted voting). At or above it, the
      attacker's resource can start winning those votes.
    </Step>
    <Step label="A security budget turns 'expensive' into 'deterred'">
      An attack is priced out once its cost exceeds what the attacker is rationally willing to spend —
      not because the attack becomes impossible, but because it stops being worth it.
    </Step>
  </Steps>

  <Variables>
    <Variable>**share** — the fraction of the network's total resource (hash power or stake) an attacker needs.</Variable>
    <Variable>**totalValue** — the resource's total value across the whole honest network; a bigger network is a more expensive target.</Variable>
  </Variables>
</Formalize>

<Play>
  <PlayDemo />

  Slide the attacker's targeted share and watch both bars grow linearly — the cost formula doesn't care
  what mechanism backs the resource, only how much of it there is in total and what fraction is being
  bought.
</Play>

<WorkedExample>
  Fixed scenario: total proof-of-work resource value \$1,000,000; total staked value \$2,000,000.

  <Steps>
    <Step label="A Sybil attack, priced">
      1,000 fake identities in a naive P2P network: `sybilCost(1000) = 1000 × 0`, which is \$0.
    </Step>
    <Step label="A 51% proof-of-work attack">
      `attackCost(0.51, 1000000)` $= 0.51 \times 1{,}000{,}000 = 510{,}000$, i.e. \$510,000, against
      the \$1,000,000 proof-of-work total.
    </Step>
    <Step label="A 51% proof-of-stake attack">
      `attackCost(0.51, 2000000)` $= 0.51 \times 2{,}000{,}000 = 1{,}020{,}000$, i.e. \$1,020,000,
      against the \$2,000,000 staked total — twice as expensive here, simply because this scenario's
      total staked value is twice the proof-of-work total.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Against a $500,000 security budget">
      - Proof-of-work: \$510,000 is more than \$500,000 → priced out
      - Proof-of-stake: \$1,020,000 is more than \$500,000 → priced out (even pricier)
      - Sybil: \$0 never exceeds any budget → never priced out — exactly why identity alone was
        never the thing worth defending
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</WorkedExample>

<Summary>
  $$
  \text{attackCost}(\text{share}, \text{totalValue}) = \text{share} \times \text{totalValue}
  $$

  Sybil resistance doesn't come from making identities hard to create — it comes from making *influence*
  cost something scarce, so that no number of free identities adds up to real power. The capstone chapter
  puts every piece from this Part together: hashing, Merkle roots, and proof-of-work mining, chained into
  an actual toy blockchain.
</Summary>
