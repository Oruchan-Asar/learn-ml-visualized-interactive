<Hook>
  Three processes, no shared clock, no synchronized watches. Given two events — one on P0, one on P2 — can you
  even say which happened first?
</Hook>

<Intuition>
  <IntuitionDemo />

  Step through nine events across three processes. Every process keeps a counter of its own — bumped by one for
  every event — except a **receive**, which first jumps up to catch the sender's counter before continuing.
  Watch each process's three-number vector grow as messages cross between them.
</Intuition>

<Formalize>
  A **Lamport clock** gives every event a single number: bump your own counter, and on a receive, jump to
  $\max(\text{own}, \text{sender's})$ first.

  $$
  L(e) = \begin{cases} L_p + 1 & e \text{ is internal or a send} \\ \max(L_p, L_{msg}) + 1 & e \text{ is a receive} \end{cases}
  $$

  A **vector clock** keeps one counter *per process* instead of one shared number: bump only your own component,
  but on receive take the componentwise max with the sender's stamped vector first.

  <Variables>
    <Variable>**$L_p$** — process $p$'s own Lamport counter, just before this event.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$L_{msg}$** — the Lamport timestamp attached to the message being received.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$a \le b$** — every component of vector $a$ is $\le$ the matching component of $b$.</Variable>
  </Variables>

  <Steps>
    <Step label="Comparing two vector clocks">
      $a$ happened-before $b$ exactly when $a \le b$ componentwise and $a \neq b$. If neither vector dominates the
      other, the events are <strong>concurrent</strong> — no message chain links them in either direction.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Why a vector, not just a number">
      A single Lamport number can always be compared to any other — but that comparison doesn't mean one event
      caused the other. A vector clock's componentwise structure is what actually distinguishes "definitely
      before" from "no relation at all."
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</Formalize>

<Play>
  <PlayDemo />

  Pick any two of the nine events. `compareVectorClocks` reads their vectors directly and reports **before**,

</Play>

<WorkedExample>
  Trace the first four events by hand: e1 (P0, internal), e2 (P0, sends m1), e3 (P0, internal), e4 (P1, receives m1).

  <Steps>
    <Step label="e1 — P0's first event">
      P0 bumps its own counter: Lamport = 1, vector = [1, 0, 0].
    </Step>
    <Step label="e2 — P0 sends m1">
      Another internal bump on P0: Lamport = 2, vector = [2, 0, 0]. This vector rides along inside m1.
    </Step>
    <Step label="e3 — P0 does more local work">
      Bump again: Lamport = 3, vector = [3, 0, 0].
    </Step>
    <Step label="e4 — P1 receives m1">
      P1 first takes the componentwise max of its own [0,0,0] and m1's stamped [2,0,0] → [2,0,0], then bumps its
      own component: vector = [2, 1, 0]. Lamport jumps to $\max(0, 2) + 1 = 3$ — coincidentally equal to e3's
      Lamport number, even though e3 and e4 are on different processes entirely.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</WorkedExample>

<Summary>
  $$
  a \to b \iff a \le b \text{ componentwise}, \quad a \neq b
  $$

  A Lamport clock hands every event a single, always-comparable number. A vector clock hands every event a
  per-process vector — and only *that* structure can tell "happened-before" apart from "no relation at all." The
  next chapter makes that gap precise.
</Summary>
