<Hook>
  Two miners find a valid block at nearly the same instant. Half the network sees one first, half sees
  the other — for a moment, there are two "true" histories. How does the network converge back to one,
  and when can you actually trust a block won't be undone?
</Hook>

<Intuition>
  <IntuitionDemo />

  Extend either chain and watch the "canonical" tag jump to whichever fork is longer. There's no vote, no
  authority deciding — the rule is mechanical: more blocks wins, full stop. A fork that falls behind
  doesn't get argued away; it just stops being extended, because no rational miner keeps building on the
  losing side.
</Intuition>

<Formalize>
  <Steps>
    <Step label="A fork is just two chains sharing a prefix">
      Both Chain A and Chain B in this chapter start from the same genesis block, then diverge —
      each new block hashes in the previous block's hash, so a fork is nothing more than two different
      sequences of blocks after the split point.
    </Step>
    <Step label="The longest chain is canonical">
      $\text{longestChain}(\{\text{chains}\}) = \arg\max_c \; \text{length}(c)$ (ties have no winner yet).
      Real chains compare cumulative proof-of-work, not raw block count, but with one difficulty target
      shared by everyone, the two are the same thing.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Finality is a probability, not a guarantee">
      A block already has $z$ **confirmations** once $z$ further blocks are built on top of it. An
      attacker controlling a fraction $q$ of the network's power can still try to grow a secret,
      longer fork in secret and replace it — but the odds fall off fast as $z$ grows.
    </Step>
    <Step label="The reversal probability shrinks exponentially in confirmations">
      $P_{\text{reverse}}(z) = \left(\dfrac{q}{1-q}\right)^{z}$ — every additional confirmation multiplies
      the attacker's chances by the same ratio $q/(1-q)$, which is less than 1 whenever the attacker
      controls a minority ($q < 0.5$) of the network.
    </Step>
  </Steps>

  <Variables>
    <Variable>**$z$** — confirmations: how many blocks are stacked on top of the one in question.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$q$** — the attacker's share of total mining power; $q/(1-q)$ is the ratio the probability shrinks by per confirmation.</Variable>
  </Variables>
</Formalize>

<Play>
  <PlayDemo />

  Drag along the curve to add confirmations and watch the reversal probability collapse. This is why
  "wait for N confirmations" is a real, if informal, security parameter: N isn't chosen because reversal
  becomes impossible, but because it becomes vanishingly, practically impossible.
</Play>

<WorkedExample>
  Chain A: 3 blocks (`a1`→216, `a2`→14, `a3`→883). Chain B: 2 blocks (`b1`→247, `b2`→399). Attacker share
  $q=0.2$.

  <Steps>
    <Step label="Which chain is canonical right now">
      $\text{length}(A)=3 > \text{length}(B)=2$, so Chain A is canonical — even though Chain B's blocks
      are individually just as validly hashed.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Chain B catches up and passes">
      If Chain B mines two more blocks (length 4), the rule flips: $4 > 3$ means Chain B is now
      canonical, and every honest node that was building on Chain A discards those blocks and switches.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Reversal probability at z = 2 confirmations">
      $\left(\dfrac{0.2}{0.8}\right)^{2} = 0.25^{2} = 0.0625$ — a 1-in-16 chance the attacker still
      catches up, still too risky to treat as final.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Reversal probability at z = 3 confirmations">
      $0.25^{3} = 0.015625$ — this is the first depth where the probability drops under this chapter's
      finality threshold of $0.02$. Two confirmations weren't enough; three are.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</WorkedExample>

<Summary>
  $$
  P_{\text{reverse}}(z) = \left(\frac{q}{1-q}\right)^{z}
  $$

  A fork resolves the moment one side pulls ahead in length — no vote required, just miners rationally
  building on whichever chain is winning. "Finality" is the point where reversing a block has become so
  improbable that treating it as permanent is a safe bet, not a mathematical certainty. Everything so far
  has assumed honest majority mining; the next chapters look at what a network built entirely out of code
  — smart contracts — does once that consensus object is settled.
</Summary>
