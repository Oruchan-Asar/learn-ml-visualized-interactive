<Hook>
  Three nodes all want the same critical section right now. There's no shared lock, no central arbiter — just
  messages. Who goes first, and how do the other two know to wait?
</Hook>

<Intuition>
  <IntuitionDemo />

  Drag each side's timestamp. Whichever request has the lower timestamp wins — and if they're ever tied, the
  lower node id breaks the tie. That's the entire ordering rule, before any protocol is built on top of it.
</Intuition>

<Formalize>
  Every request carries a Lamport timestamp. One request has **priority** over another by comparing them
  lexicographically:

  $$
  (\text{ts}_a, \text{id}_a) < (\text{ts}_b, \text{id}_b)
  $$

  <Variables>
    <Variable>**$\text{ts}$** — the requester's Lamport timestamp when it asked for the critical section.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$\text{id}$** — the requesting node's id, used only to break exact timestamp ties.</Variable>
  </Variables>

  **Ricart-Agrawala** turns that ordering into a protocol: broadcast a request stamped with your timestamp, then
  enter the critical section only once every other node has replied.

  <Steps>
    <Step label="When to reply immediately">
      If you're not in the critical section, and either you're not requesting it yourself or the incoming
      request has priority over your own, reply right away — you have no claim to go first.
    </Step>
    <Step label="When to defer">
      If you're already in the critical section, or you're requesting it and your own request has priority,
      hold the reply until you're done. The requester simply waits.
    </Step>
    <Step label="An alternative: pass a token">
      A **token ring** sidesteps voting entirely — a single token circulates node to node in a fixed cycle, and
      only whoever holds it may enter. Mutual exclusion falls out for free: there's only ever one token.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</Formalize>

<Play>
  <PlayDemo />

  Three simultaneous requests — P (ts 5), Q (ts 3), R (ts 3, tied with Q). The reply matrix shows exactly who
  defers to whom, and `determineEntryOrder` reports the single global order that falls out of it. Below, pass the
  token around the ring by hand.
</Play>

<WorkedExample>
  P requests at timestamp 5, Q at 3, R at 3 (tied with Q).

  <Steps>
    <Step label="Break the Q/R tie">
      Q and R both requested at timestamp 3, so node id decides: "Q" sorts before "R", so Q has priority over R.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Full entry order">
      Sorting all three by (timestamp, id):

      1. Q (3, Q)
      2. R (3, R)
      3. P (5, P)

      Matching `determineEntryOrder`'s output exactly.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Why Q defers to everyone">
      Q has priority over both P and R, so by the reply rule, Q *defers* replying to either of them until Q has
      finished — Q is the one who should go first, so nobody should be told to proceed ahead of it.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Why P replies to everyone">
      P has priority over no one (5 loses to both 3's), so P has nothing to gain by holding up its replies — it
      answers Q and R immediately.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</WorkedExample>

<DistributedMutualExclusionCheckpoint />

<Summary>
  $$
  (\text{ts}_a, \text{id}_a) < (\text{ts}_b, \text{id}_b)
  $$

  Without a shared lock, a node still needs a rule for "who goes first" that every other node will agree with —
  Ricart-Agrawala gets there by comparing (timestamp, id) pairs and deferring replies accordingly; a token ring
  gets there by making "who's allowed in" a physical fact about who's currently holding a single token.
</Summary>
