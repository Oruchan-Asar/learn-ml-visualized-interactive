</Hook>

<Formalize>
  Naive Bayes multiplies Bayes' rule across every feature, assuming they're independent *given the class* — the "naive" part:

  $$
  P(\text{class} \mid x_1,\dots,x_n) \propto P(\text{class}) \prod_{i=1}^n P(x_i \mid \text{class})
  $$

  <Variables>
    <Variable>**class** — the category being predicted, such as spam or not-spam.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$x_1,\dots,x_n$** — the $n$ individual pieces of evidence, such as which words are present.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$P(\text{class})$** — the prior probability of the class, before looking at any evidence.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$P(x_i \mid \text{class})$** — the likelihood of feature $i$: how often it shows up in training examples of that class.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$n$** — the number of features being combined.</Variable>
  </Variables>

  Each $P(x_i \mid \text{class})$ is just a frequency: how often word $i$ showed up in training messages of that class. Multiplying independent probabilities together is exactly what "assume independence" *means* — it's the one assumption that turns a hard joint-probability problem into a handful of easy word-counting ones.
</Formalize>

  <PlayDemo />

  Build a message by toggling which words are present. "Meeting" alone should pull hard toward not-spam; "free" alone pulls the other way. Watch how the posterior shifts as you add or remove one piece of evidence at a time — each word's presence (or absence) multiplies in independently.

<WorkedExample>
  "Free" never appears in any of the three not-spam training messages:

  <Steps>
    <Step label="The zero-frequency problem">
      $P(\text{free}\mid\text{not spam}) = 0/3 = 0$ exactly. For a message containing "free," this makes the
      *entire* not-spam probability collapse to precisely $0$ — not small, not unlikely, but mathematically
      impossible — purely because of one gap in a tiny training set.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Laplace smoothing patches it">
      Add one fake "seen" and one fake "not seen" example to every count: $P(\text{free}\mid\text{not spam})
      = (0+1)/(3+2) = 0.2$. Never exactly zero again, no matter how sparse the training data.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Same prediction, honest confidence">
      For "free, money": the unsmoothed model claims $100\%$ spam, $0\%$ not-spam — absolute certainty from
      one missing example. The smoothed model reaches the *same* correct prediction, spam, but at a far more
      reasonable $90\%$ to $10\%$.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</WorkedExample>

<Summary>
  $$
  P(\text{class} \mid x_1,\dots,x_n) \propto P(\text{class}) \prod_{i=1}^n P(x_i \mid \text{class})
  $$

  The independence assumption is almost always technically false — whether an email says "free" and whether it says "money" are not really independent — and naive Bayes works remarkably well anyway, because getting the relative ranking between classes right matters far more than getting the exact probabilities right. Laplace smoothing isn't a minor implementation detail; without it, any word that happens to be missing from one class's tiny training set can make an entire prediction mathematically impossible, forever.
</Summary>
