<Hook>
  Part II's linear regression fit a line through a scatter of points. What happens when the points come one after another in time, and the "noise" the line doesn't explain isn't noise at all — it's a pattern that repeats every few steps?
</Hook>

<Intuition>
  <IntuitionDemo />

  Eight points, rising overall but wobbling up and down along the way. The straight line is Part II's ordinary least squares, fit directly on the raw numbers — it captures the rise, but leaves a suspiciously regular zig-zag of leftover error behind.
</Intuition>

<Formalize>
  Time series decomposition splits a series into a trend and a repeating **season**:

  $$
  y_t = \text{trend}(t) + \text{season}(t \bmod p)
  $$

  Fit the trend first — ordinary least squares, same as always. Then look at what's left over: the **residuals**, $y_t - \text{trend}(t)$. Average those residuals by their position in the cycle (every 4th one, if the period is 4), and that average *is* the seasonal component — the part of the pattern that keeps repeating.
</Formalize>

<Play>
  <PlayDemo />

  Add the fitted season back on top of the fitted trend, and the reconstruction passes through every single observed point exactly. Nothing about this series was ever "noisy" — it was two clean, additive patterns that a straight line alone could never see on its own.
</Play>

<WorkedExample>
  Eight points: a true trend of $2t+10$ plus a true season of $(3,-3,-3,3)$ repeating every 4 steps:

  <Steps>
    <Step label="Fit the trend on the raw series">
      Ordinary least squares recovers slope $=2$ and intercept $=10$ *exactly* — this particular season
      happens to be orthogonal to the trend over these 8 points, so it doesn't bias the fit at all. That's a
      property of this specific data, not something you'd get for free in general.
    </Step>
    <Step label="The residuals are the season">
      Subtracting the fitted trend from each point leaves exactly $(3,-3,-3,3,3,-3,-3,3)$ — the true season,
      recovered with no error, because the trend fit had none either.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Forecasting past the data">
      At $t=11$ (phase $3$, one full cycle past the last observed point): trend $=2(11)+10=32$, season
      $=+3$, forecast $=35$ — a real prediction for a time step that was never observed.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</WorkedExample>

<Summary>
  $$
  y_t = \text{trend}(t) + \text{season}(t \bmod p)
  $$

  This chapter's trend and season happened to separate perfectly — in real data they rarely do, and a trend fit on raw, seasonal data is usually at least a little biased by the very pattern it's supposed to ignore. Real forecasting methods (like ARIMA, or repeating this fit-and-subtract process iteratively) exist mostly to handle that imperfection. But the core idea — split a signal into parts, fit each part with tools already in this course, add them back together — is exactly what's happening here, at the smallest possible scale.
</Summary>
