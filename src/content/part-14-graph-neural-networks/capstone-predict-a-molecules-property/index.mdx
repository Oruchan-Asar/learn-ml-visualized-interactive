<Formalize>
  This capstone chains the last two chapters' rules into a single two-layer pipeline, then reduces the
  result to one number:

  $$
  h^{(1)} = \text{GCN}(h^{(0)}), \qquad h^{(2)} = \text{GAT}(h^{(1)}), \qquad \hat{y} = \sum_v h^{(2)}_v
  $$

  Layer 1 is Chapter 2's plain neighborhood mean — every atom blends with its bonded neighbors, no
  weighting. Layer 2 is last chapter's attention rule, applied *on top of* layer 1's output, not the raw
  features — so by the time attention runs, every value already carries a first hop of neighbor
  information. The final sum — a **readout** — is the simplest way to turn six per-atom numbers into one
  whole-molecule prediction: real molecular property predictors do exactly this, summing (or averaging)
  learned atom embeddings into a single graph-level output.
</Formalize>

<WorkedExample>
  Atom $0$ on the **ring** structure, through both layers:

  <Steps>
    <Step label="Layer 1 (GCN): mean over atom 0's neighborhood {0, 1, 2}">
      $h^{(1)}_0 = \frac{1+2+0}{3} = 1.0$. The same round also gives $h^{(1)}_1 = 1.5$ and
      $h^{(1)}_2 = 1.0$ — needed next, since layer 2 attends over *these* values, not the raw ones.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Layer 2 (GAT): attention over the same neighborhood, using layer 1's output">
      Scores: $e_{0,0}=0$, $e_{0,1}=\text{LeakyReLU}(1.5-1.0)=0.5$, $e_{0,2}=\text{LeakyReLU}(1.0-1.0)=0$.
      Softmax gives weights $\approx(0.274,\ 0.452,\ 0.274)$ — atom $1$ dominates, since it's the only
      neighbor layer 1 pushed higher than atom $0$ itself.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Atom 0's final embedding">
      $h^{(2)}_0 \approx 0.274(1.0) + 0.452(1.5) + 0.274(1.0) \approx 1.226$. Repeat this for all six
      atoms and sum the results, and the ring's whole-molecule prediction comes out to about
      $10.278$ — versus about $9.250$ for the exact same atoms wired into a chain instead.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</WorkedExample>

<Summary>
  $$
  \hat{y} = \sum_v \text{GAT}\big(\text{GCN}(h^{(0)})\big)_v
  $$

  Two layers, two different rules, one readout — and the atoms never had to change for the prediction to
  change. That's the entire reason this part exists: a molecule's properties depend on its *bonds*, not
  just its atoms, and a plain feedforward network fed only atom features has no way to see that at all.
  Real molecular GNNs stack more layers, learn every weight instead of fixing them by hand, and use richer
  readouts — but the shape of the pipeline (message passing, then attention, then pooling to one number)
  is exactly what's here. Part XV turns to a different structural limitation entirely: what happens when
  the "neighbors" aren't a handful of bonded atoms, but every previous token in a sequence thousands of
  positions long.
</Summary>
