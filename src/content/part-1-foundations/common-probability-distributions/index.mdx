<Hook>
  A coin flip, a count of rare events, a bell curve, a probability vector that's itself uncertain — four
  completely different-looking situations, and machine learning reaches for a named distribution to model
  every one of them.
</Hook>

<Intuition>
  <IntuitionDemo />

  Toggle between the four. Each one has an exact formula behind it, and every bar (or curve point) on screen
  is that formula evaluated at a specific value — nothing here is approximated or simulated.
</Intuition>

<Formalize>
  Each distribution answers a different shape of question:

  $$
  \text{Bernoulli: } P(X{=}1)=p \qquad \text{Poisson: } P(X{=}k)=\frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k}{k!} \qquad
  \text{Gaussian: } f(x)=\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}
  $$

  <Variables>
    <Variable>**$p$** — the Bernoulli's single parameter: the probability of outcome 1.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$\lambda$** — the Poisson's rate parameter: the expected count.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$\mu, \sigma$** — the Gaussian's mean and standard deviation.</Variable>
  </Variables>

  A **Dirichlet** distribution is one level up: instead of putting mass on numbers, it puts mass on
  *probability vectors themselves*. Its expected vector has a strikingly simple form:

  $$
  \mathbb{E}[p_i] = \frac{\alpha_i}{\sum_j \alpha_j}
  $$

  <Variables>
    <Variable>**$\alpha_i$** — the concentration parameter for category $i$ (a pseudo-count of prior observations).</Variable>
    <Variable>**$\mathbb{E}[p_i]$** — the expected probability the Dirichlet assigns to category $i$.</Variable>
  </Variables>

  <Steps>
    <Step label="Bernoulli is the atom">
      Every other discrete distribution here is built by combining or generalizing Bernoulli trials.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Poisson counts, Gaussian measures">
      Poisson lives on non-negative integers (counts); the Gaussian lives on the whole real line
      (measurements) and is fully determined by just its first two moments.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Dirichlet is a distribution over distributions">
      Where Bernoulli/Poisson/Gaussian describe one random *number*, the Dirichlet describes an entire
      random *categorical distribution* — exactly the object a classifier's softmax output is trying to
      estimate.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</Formalize>

<Play>
  <PlayDemo />

  Poisson's bars and the Gaussian's curve both come from a single closed-form line of code — the same
  determinism that made the coin-flip Bernoulli exact also makes $e^{-2}\lambda^k/k!$ and the bell-curve
  formula exact, just with more decimal places to track by hand.
</Play>

<WorkedExample>
  <Steps>
    <Step label="Poisson(λ=2): compute P(X=0)">
      $$
      P(X{=}0) = \frac{e^{-2}\cdot 2^0}{0!} = e^{-2} \approx 0.1353
      $$
    </Step>
    <Step label="Poisson(λ=2): compute P(X=1) and P(X=2) — and notice they tie">
      $$
      P(X{=}1) = \frac{e^{-2}\cdot 2^1}{1!} = 2e^{-2} \approx 0.2707 \qquad
      P(X{=}2) = \frac{e^{-2}\cdot 2^2}{2!} = \frac{4e^{-2}}{2} = 2e^{-2} \approx 0.2707
      $$
      When $\lambda$ is a whole number, $P(X{=}\lambda{-}1)$ and $P(X{=}\lambda)$ are always exactly equal —
      the distribution has two tied modes.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Dirichlet(α=[2,3,5]): compute the expected vector">
      $$
      \mathbb{E}[p] = \left[\frac{2}{10}, \frac{3}{10}, \frac{5}{10}\right] = [0.2,\ 0.3,\ 0.5]
      $$
      The concentration parameters' own proportions become the expected probabilities — no extra machinery
      needed.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</WorkedExample>

<Summary>
  $$
  P(X{=}1)=p \qquad P(X{=}k)=\frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k}{k!} \qquad
  f(x)=\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}} \qquad \mathbb{E}[p_i]=\frac{\alpha_i}{\sum_j\alpha_j}
  $$

  Almost every probabilistic model in this course is one of these four shapes wearing a different hat: a
  classifier's output layer is Bernoulli or categorical, a count-based likelihood is Poisson, a continuous
  noise model is Gaussian, and a prior over class probabilities is Dirichlet.
</Summary>
