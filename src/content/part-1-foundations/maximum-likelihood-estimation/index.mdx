<Hook>
  You flip a coin a handful of times and see mostly heads. What single value of "probability of heads"
  makes the data you actually saw the *least surprising*?
</Hook>

<Intuition>
  <IntuitionDemo />

  Drag the candidate $p$ across the curve. Every point on it is the log-likelihood of these exact five
  flips under that one value of $p$ — and the curve has a single, unambiguous high point.
</Intuition>

<Formalize>
  For $n$ independent Bernoulli trials with $k$ heads, the likelihood of the data under parameter $p$ is

  $$
  L(p) = p^k (1-p)^{n-k}
  $$

  <Variables>
    <Variable>**$L(p)$** — the likelihood: how probable the observed data is, as a function of $p$.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$k$** — the number of heads actually observed.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$n$** — the total number of flips.</Variable>
  </Variables>

  Products of small numbers underflow fast, so in practice everyone maximizes the **log-likelihood** instead
  — same maximizer, better numerics:

  $$
  \ell(p) = \log L(p) = k\log p + (n-k)\log(1-p)
  $$

  <Steps>
    <Step label="The log is monotonic, so the peak doesn't move">
      $\log$ is strictly increasing, so whatever $p$ maximizes $L(p)$ also maximizes $\ell(p)$ — taking the
      log never changes *where* the maximum sits.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Setting the derivative to zero gives a closed form">
      $$
      \frac{d\ell}{dp} = \frac{k}{p} - \frac{n-k}{1-p} = 0
      $$

      Move the second term across and cross-multiply: $\frac{k}{p} = \frac{n-k}{1-p} \implies k(1-p) =
      p(n-k)$. Expand both sides: $k - kp = pn - pk$. The $-kp$ on the left and $-pk$ on the right are the
      same term, so they cancel, leaving $k = pn$, so

      $$
      \hat{p} = \frac{k}{n}
      $$

      — the maximum likelihood estimate is exactly the observed proportion of heads, no search required.
    </Step>
    <Step label="This is the general MLE recipe">
      Write the likelihood of the data under a parameter, take its log, and find where the derivative
      vanishes — the same three steps work for far more complex models than a coin.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</Formalize>

<Play>
  <PlayDemo />

  The curve's exact peak sits at $p=0.8$, matching $\hat{p}=k/n$ exactly — drag away in either direction and
  the log-likelihood only gets worse, confirming this closed form really is the unique maximizer.
</Play>

<WorkedExample>
  Five flips: H, H, T, H, H — so $k=4$ heads out of $n=5$.

  <Steps>
    <Step label="Apply the closed-form MLE">
      $$
      \hat{p} = \frac{k}{n} = \frac{4}{5} = 0.8
      $$
    </Step>
    <Step label="Confirm it by evaluating the log-likelihood at the estimate">
      $$
      \ell(0.8) = 4\log(0.8) + 1\log(0.2) \approx 4(-0.2231) + (-1.6094) \approx -2.502
      $$
    </Step>
    <Step label="Check a nearby value scores worse">
      $$
      \ell(0.6) = 4\log(0.6) + 1\log(0.4) \approx 4(-0.5108) + (-0.9163) \approx -2.960
      $$
      Worse (more negative) than $-2.502$ — exactly what "$0.8$ is the maximizer" predicts.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</WorkedExample>

<MleCheckpoint />

<Summary>
  $$
  \hat{p} = \frac{k}{n} = \arg\max_p \big[k\log p + (n-k)\log(1-p)\big]
  $$

  Maximum likelihood estimation is this same idea at any scale: write down how probable your data is under a
  parameter, then find the parameter that makes that probability as large as possible. Logistic regression,
  neural network training via cross-entropy, and Gaussian curve-fitting are all MLE in disguise.
</Summary>
