<Hook>
  A matrix bends almost every direction you feed it into something new. But "almost" is doing real
  work in that sentence — are there directions a given matrix leaves alone, only stretching them,
  never rotating them at all?
</Hook>

<Intuition>
  <IntuitionDemo />

  Drag $v$ around. For most positions, $Av$ points somewhere completely different from $v$ — the
  rotation angle swings all over the place. But near two particular directions, that angle collapses
  toward $0°$: $A$ stops rotating and only scales.
</Intuition>

<Formalize>
  A vector $v$ is an **eigenvector** of $A$ if $A$ doesn't rotate it at all — only scales it:

  $$
  Av = \lambda v
  $$

  <Variables>
    <Variable>**$A$** — the matrix being analyzed.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$v$** — the eigenvector: a direction $A$ only scales, never rotates.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$\lambda$** — the eigenvalue: how much that direction gets stretched (or flipped, if negative).</Variable>
  </Variables>

  <Steps>
    <Step label="Finding lambda first">
      Rearranging gives $(A-\lambda I)v = 0$, which has a nonzero solution $v$ only when
      $\det(A-\lambda I)=0$ — a polynomial in $\lambda$ called the **characteristic polynomial**. Its
      roots are exactly the eigenvalues.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Then solving for each eigenvector">
      Once $\lambda$ is known, $(A-\lambda I)v=0$ becomes a system with a whole line of solutions —
      any nonzero vector along that line is a valid eigenvector for that eigenvalue.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Eigenvectors need not be perpendicular">
      Unlike a symmetric matrix, a general matrix's eigenvectors don't have to be at right angles to
      each other — this chapter's example has eigenvectors at $(1,1)$ and $(1,-2)$, which are not
      orthogonal.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</Formalize>

<Play>
  <PlayDemo />

  Watch the stretch ratio $|Av|/|v|$ alongside the rotation angle. At each of the two special
  directions, the ratio locks onto a fixed number — that number *is* $\lambda$ for that direction, no
  matter how far out you drag $v$ along it.
</Play>

<WorkedExample>
  For $A = \begin{pmatrix}4&1\\2&3\end{pmatrix}$:

  <Steps>
    <Step label="Solve the characteristic equation">
      $\det(A-\lambda I) = (4-\lambda)(3-\lambda) - (1)(2) = \lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 10 = (\lambda-5)(\lambda-2) = 0$,
      so $\lambda_1=5$ and $\lambda_2=2$.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Solve for the first eigenvector">
      For $\lambda_1=5$: $(A-5I)v=0 \Rightarrow -x+y=0 \Rightarrow y=x$, giving direction $(1,1)$. Check:
      $A(1,1) = (4+1, 2+3) = (5,5) = 5(1,1)$. ✓
    </Step>
    <Step label="Solve for the second eigenvector">
      For $\lambda_2=2$: $(A-2I)v=0 \Rightarrow 2x+y=0 \Rightarrow y=-2x$, giving direction $(1,-2)$. Check:
      $A(1,-2) = (4-2, 2-6) = (2,-4) = 2(1,-2)$. ✓
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</WorkedExample>

<Summary>
  $$
  Av = \lambda v
  $$

  Every square matrix carries its own set of invariant directions — the eigenvectors — each with its
  own private scale factor, the eigenvalue. Finding them means solving one polynomial and then one
  linear system per root, no guessing required. Later chapters build directly on this: positive
  semi-definite matrices are defined by the sign of their eigenvalues, and the SVD generalizes this
  exact machinery to matrices that aren't even square.
</Summary>
