<Hook>
  A test comes back positive. How likely is it that you actually have the condition — really?
</Hook>

<Intuition>

  Each square is one of 100 people. Teal means they have the condition and tested positive; ochre means they don't have it but tested positive anyway. Drag the base rate down — watch the ochre squares start to outnumber the teal ones, even though the test itself hasn't changed at all.

<Formalize>
  $P(A\mid B)$ reads "the probability of $A$, given that $B$ happened" — your belief in $A$ *updated* by evidence $B$. **Bayes' rule** says how to compute it —

  $$
  P(A\mid B) = \frac{P(B\mid A)\,P(A)}{P(B)}
  $$

  <Variables>
    <Variable>**$A$** — the event we want to know about (e.g., "has the condition").</Variable>
    <Variable>**$B$** — the observed evidence (e.g., "tested positive").</Variable>
    <Variable>**$P(A\mid B)$** — the updated probability of $A$ given that $B$ happened (the posterior).</Variable>
    <Variable>**$P(B\mid A)$** — the probability of seeing evidence $B$ if $A$ is true (the likelihood, e.g. sensitivity).</Variable>
    <Variable>**$P(A)$** — the probability of $A$ before seeing any evidence (the prior, or base rate).</Variable>
    <Variable>**$P(B)$** — the overall probability of observing $B$, across all cases.</Variable>
  </Variables>

  Here $A$ is "has the condition," $B$ is "tested positive." $P(B\mid A)$ is the test's sensitivity — how often it catches a real case. $P(A)$ is the base rate you just dragged.
</Formalize>

<Play>
  <PlayDemo />

  Watch $P(A\mid B)$ track the base rate: the test's accuracy never changes, yet how much a positive result should actually convince you depends heavily on how rare the condition was to begin with.
</Play>

<WorkedExample>
  Base rate $1\%$, sensitivity $90\%$, false-positive rate $10\%$:

  <Steps>
    <Step label="Find the overall chance of a positive test">
      $$
      P(B) = 0.9(0.01) + 0.1(0.99) = 0.108
      $$
    </Step>
    <Step label="Apply Bayes' rule">
      $$
      P(A\mid B) = \frac{0.9 \times 0.01}{0.108} \approx 8.3\%
      $$
    </Step>
    <Step label="Read what it means">
      A "90% accurate" test, and a positive result still means less than a 1-in-10 chance of actually having it —
      because the condition was rare enough that false alarms from the other 99% swamp the true positives.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</WorkedExample>

<Summary>
  $$
  P(A\mid B) = \frac{P(B\mid A)\,P(A)}{P(B)}
  $$

  A test's own accuracy isn't the whole story — how rare the thing you're testing for is changes what a positive result actually means. Bayes' rule is the exact accounting for that.
</Summary>
