<Hook>
  You've applied the chain rule to one function composed with another. Every framework you'll use from here
  on applies it automatically, to graphs with thousands of nodes. What does that automation actually have to
  do, node by node?
</Hook>

<Intuition>
  <IntuitionDemo />

  This is the entire computational graph for $f(a,b)=a\cdot b + a$: two inputs, one multiply node, one add
  node. Tune $a$ and $b$ and watch every node's value update — that's the **forward pass**, nothing more than
  evaluating the graph in order.
</Intuition>

<Formalize>
  The **backward pass** seeds the output's gradient at 1, then walks the graph in reverse, applying one local
  rule at each node:

  $$
  \frac{\partial L}{\partial u} \mathrel{+}= \frac{\partial L}{\partial v}\cdot\frac{\partial v}{\partial u}
  $$

  <Variables>
    <Variable>**$v$** — a node, already visited, whose incoming gradient $\partial L/\partial v$ is known.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$u$** — one of $v$'s inputs, receiving a contribution to its own gradient.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$\partial v/\partial u$** — $v$'s *local* derivative with respect to that one input: $1$ for a sum, the *other* input's value for a product.</Variable>
  </Variables>

  <Steps>
    <Step label="This is the chain rule, applied one node at a time">
      Nothing here is new — it's the same chain rule from Chapter 3, just applied locally at every node
      instead of algebraically for the whole expression at once.
    </Step>
    <Step label="+= matters: a node used twice accumulates">
      If a value feeds two different downstream nodes, its total gradient is the *sum* of what flows back
      from each path — never just one of them.
    </Step>
    <Step label="A product node's local rule swaps its inputs">
      For $v=u_1\cdot u_2$, $\partial v/\partial u_1 = u_2$ and $\partial v/\partial u_2=u_1$ — each input's
      local derivative is literally the forward-pass value of the *other* one.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</Formalize>

<Play>
  <PlayDemo />

  Toggle to "Backward pass" and watch the same graph now display gradients instead of values — and notice
  the readout cross-checks every one of them against a plain numerical (finite-difference) gradient, which
  never even looks at the graph.
</Play>

<WorkedExample>
  Run both passes on $f(a,b)=a\cdot b+a$ at $a=3, b=4$, using the graph $n_1=a\cdot b$, $n_2=n_1+a$ (the output):

  <Steps>
    <Step label="Forward pass">
      $$
      n_1 = 3\times4=12, \qquad n_2 = 12+3=15
      $$
    </Step>
    <Step label="Seed the output gradient and cross the add node">
      $\partial f/\partial n_2 = 1$. The add node $n_2=n_1+a$ passes its incoming gradient through unchanged
      to *both* inputs: gradient into $n_1$ is $1$, and $a$ already receives a direct contribution of $1$.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Cross the multiply node">
      $n_1=a\cdot b$: the gradient arriving at $n_1$ (which is $1$) distributes as $1\times b=4$ to $a$, and
      $1\times a=3$ to $b$.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Sum every path into a">
      $a$ received $1$ from the direct add-path and $4$ from the multiply-path: $\partial f/\partial
      a=1+4=5$. And $\partial f/\partial b=3$, with no second path to add. Both match the closed-form
      derivative, $\partial f/\partial a = b+1=5$ and $\partial f/\partial b=a=3$, exactly.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</WorkedExample>

<AutogradCheckpoint />

<Summary>
  $$
  \frac{\partial L}{\partial u} \mathrel{+}= \frac{\partial L}{\partial v}\cdot\frac{\partial v}{\partial u}
  $$

  That's the whole engine: a forward pass that evaluates every node once, and a backward pass that applies
  one local multiply-and-accumulate rule per node, in reverse. Real autograd systems — PyTorch, and every
  framework since — do exactly this at far larger scale, on graphs with millions of nodes instead of two.
  There's no more machinery to add; there's only more graph.
</Summary>
