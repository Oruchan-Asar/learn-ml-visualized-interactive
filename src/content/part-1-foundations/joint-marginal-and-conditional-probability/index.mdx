<Hook>
  Two variables share one probability table. Read across a row and you get one story; read down a column
  and you get another; ignore the table's structure entirely and you get a third. Which one answers your
  actual question?
</Hook>

<Intuition>
  <IntuitionDemo />

  Click a row header ($A$) or a column header ($B$). The cells you picked light up, and the readout below
  shows what's left once you renormalize just that slice — the conditional distribution living inside the
  joint table all along.
</Intuition>

<Formalize>
  A **joint** distribution $P(A,B)$ assigns a probability to every combination of outcomes at once. Summing
  a whole row or column **marginalizes out** the other variable:

  $$
  P(A=a) = \sum_b P(A=a,\,B=b)
  $$

  <Variables>
    <Variable>**$P(A,B)$** — the joint probability table: one number per (row, column) combination.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$P(A=a)$** — the marginal probability of $A=a$, found by summing across every value of $B$.</Variable>
  </Variables>

  A **conditional** distribution slices out one row or column and renormalizes it so it sums to 1 again:

  $$
  P(B\mid A=a) = \frac{P(A=a,\,B)}{P(A=a)}
  $$

  <Variables>
    <Variable>**$P(B\mid A=a)$** — the distribution over $B$, restricted to the world where $A=a$ is already known.</Variable>
  </Variables>

  <Steps>
    <Step label="Marginals are just row and column sums">
      No new arithmetic — marginalizing is exactly the sum you'd take to check a row or column adds up.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Conditioning is slicing, then renormalizing">
      Fix one variable's value, take the sub-table that survives, and divide every entry by whatever that
      sub-table currently sums to. That division is the entire difference between a joint slice and a
      conditional distribution.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Conditioning on a row and conditioning on a column don't rhyme">
      $P(B\mid A{=}a)$ divides by a row sum; $P(A\mid B{=}b)$ divides by a column sum. Nothing forces these
      two conditionals to look alike, even though they come from the same table.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</Formalize>

<Play>
  <PlayDemo />

  Pick any cell: the joint value is just that one entry, but the two conditionals built from it divide by
  completely different denominators — a row sum for $P(B\mid A)$, a column sum for $P(A\mid B)$ — which is
  exactly why $P(B\mid A) \ne P(A\mid B)$ in general.
</Play>

<WorkedExample>
  <Steps>
    <Step label="Read off the joint row for a1">
      $P(a_1, b_1)=0.2$, $P(a_1,b_2)=0.1$, $P(a_1,b_3)=0.2$ — this row sums to $P(a_1)=0.5$.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Marginalize to get P(b3)">
      Sum column $b_3$ down both rows: $P(b_3) = 0.2 + 0.1 = 0.3$.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Condition on b3 to get P(a2 | b3)">
      $$
      P(a_2 \mid b_3) = \frac{P(a_2, b_3)}{P(b_3)} = \frac{0.1}{0.3} = \frac{1}{3} \approx 0.333
      $$
      Notice this is *not* the same as $P(a_2)=0.5$ — seeing $B=b_3$ actually shifted the belief about $A$.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</WorkedExample>

<JointCheckpoint />

<Summary>
  $$
  P(A=a) = \sum_b P(A=a,B=b) \qquad P(B\mid A=a) = \frac{P(A=a,B)}{P(A=a)}
  $$

  A joint table quietly contains every marginal and every conditional distribution over its variables — sum
  a direction to marginalize, slice-and-renormalize to condition. Nearly every "what does the model actually
  believe, given what it just observed" question in probabilistic ML reduces to one of these two operations.
</Summary>
