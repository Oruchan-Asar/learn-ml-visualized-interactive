import { IntuitionDemo, PlayDemo, TaylorCheckpoint } from "./Demos";

<Hook>
  A tangent line matches a curve's value and slope at one point — and nowhere else. How much of the *actual*
  curve can you recover just by adding more information at that same single point?
</Hook>

<Intuition>
  <IntuitionDemo />

  The dashed curve is $f(x)=x^3$. The solid line is its tangent at $x_0$ — drag the slider and watch the line
  pivot, matching the curve closely right at $x_0$ but drifting away fast on either side.
</Intuition>

<Formalize>
  A **Taylor series** keeps adding derivative terms at $x_0$ to build a better local approximation:

  $$
  f(x) \approx f(x_0) + f'(x_0)(x-x_0) + \frac{f''(x_0)}{2}(x-x_0)^2 + \cdots
  $$

  <Variables>
    <Variable>**$x_0$** — the point the approximation is built around (the point of expansion).</Variable>
    <Variable>**$f(x_0)$** — the function's actual value at $x_0$; the zeroth-order term.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$f'(x_0)(x-x_0)$** — the first-order (linear) term: the tangent line's contribution.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$\dfrac{f''(x_0)}{2}(x-x_0)^2$** — the second-order (quadratic) term: adds curvature.</Variable>
  </Variables>

  <Steps>
    <Step label="Truncating after one term is the tangent line">
      Stopping after the linear term recovers exactly the tangent-line approximation from Chapter 1 — same
      formula, new name.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Adding the quadratic term adds curvature">
      The quadratic term is the first one that can bend — it uses $f''(x_0)$ to curve the approximation the
      same way the true function curves near $x_0$.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Every term after that keeps closing the gap">
      Each additional term corrects more of what the previous ones missed; for a well-behaved function, the
      approximation converges to $f(x)$ itself as more terms are added.
    </Step>
    <Step label="It only works locally">
      All of this accuracy is local — no matter how many terms you add, expect the approximation to get worse
      the farther $x$ strays from $x_0$.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</Formalize>

<Play>
  <PlayDemo />

  Move $x_0$ and the evaluation point independently. Near $x_0$, both approximations track the curve almost
  perfectly; the quadratic one keeps tracking noticeably farther out, because it bends with the curve instead
  of running straight through it.
</Play>

<WorkedExample>
  Expand $f(x)=x^3$ around $x_0=1$ and evaluate at $x=1.5$:

  <Steps>
    <Step label="Compute the ingredients at x0=1">
      $$
      f(1)=1,\qquad f'(1)=3(1)^2=3,\qquad f''(1)=6(1)=6
      $$
    </Step>
    <Step label="Build the linear approximation">
      $$
      L(1.5) = 1 + 3(0.5) = 2.5
      $$
    </Step>
    <Step label="Build the quadratic approximation">
      $$
      Q(1.5) = 1 + 3(0.5) + \frac{6}{2}(0.5)^2 = 1 + 1.5 + 0.75 = 3.25
      $$
    </Step>
    <Step label="Compare against the true value">
      $f(1.5)=3.375$.
      - The linear approximation misses by $0.875$.
      - The quadratic one misses by only $0.125$.

      Seven times closer, from one extra term.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</WorkedExample>

<Summary>
  $$
  f(x) \approx f(x_0) + f'(x_0)(x-x_0) + \frac{f''(x_0)}{2}(x-x_0)^2
  $$

  Every "linear approximation" and "quadratic approximation" you'll meet later — a linearized loss landscape,
  a locally-quadratic optimizer step — is exactly this truncated Taylor series, keeping just enough terms to
  be cheap while still capturing the curvature that matters nearby. The next chapter picks the second-order
  term back up and asks what curvature means for an entire multivariable landscape at once.
</Summary>
