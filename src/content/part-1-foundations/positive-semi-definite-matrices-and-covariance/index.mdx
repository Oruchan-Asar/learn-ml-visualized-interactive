<Hook>
  Every real dataset has a covariance matrix, and every real covariance matrix shares one strange
  property: no matter which direction you measure variance along, you never get a negative number.
  That's not a coincidence — it's baked into what "variance" means, and it has a name: **positive
  semi-definite**.
</Hook>

<Intuition>
  <IntuitionDemo />

  Each bar is $v^\top M v$ at a different direction $v$. For the covariance matrix, every bar stays on
  the positive side no matter which direction you check. Switch to the other matrix and watch one bar
  cross to the negative side — proof that matrix could never be a real covariance matrix.
</Intuition>

<Formalize>
  A symmetric matrix $M$ is **positive semi-definite (PSD)** if its quadratic form never goes negative:

  $$
  v^\top M v \geq 0 \quad \text{for every vector } v
  $$

  <Variables>
    <Variable>**$M$** — a symmetric matrix (so $M=M^\top$).</Variable>
    <Variable>**$v$** — any vector; the condition must hold for *all* of them, not just some.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$v^\top M v$** — the quadratic form: a single number measuring how $M$ acts along direction $v$.</Variable>
  </Variables>

  <Steps>
    <Step label="Why covariance matrices are always PSD">
      For a covariance matrix $C$, $v^\top C v$ is exactly the variance of the data projected onto
      direction $v$ — and variance, being an average of squared numbers, can never be negative.
    </Step>
    <Step label="What it means for the eigenvalues">
      $M$ is PSD if and only if every eigenvalue of $M$ is $\geq 0$. Along an eigenvector $v$,
      $v^\top M v = \lambda\|v\|^2$, so a negative eigenvalue immediately produces a negative quadratic
      form there.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Not every symmetric matrix qualifies">
      Symmetry alone isn't enough — a symmetric matrix with even one negative eigenvalue fails the test,
      and could never arise as an actual covariance matrix of real data.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</Formalize>

<WorkedExample>
  Four mean-centered points $(3,1), (1,3), (-3,-1), (-1,-3)$ give the covariance matrix
  $C=\begin{pmatrix}5&3\\3&5\end{pmatrix}$ (population covariance, dividing by $n=4$):

  <Steps>
    <Step label="Variance along the x-axis">
      $v=(1,0)$: $v^\top C v = 1(5\cdot1+3\cdot0)+0(\cdot) = 5$ — matches $\mathrm{Var}(x)=5$ directly.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Variance along the direction of maximum spread">
      $v=(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2},\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2})$: expanding $v^\top C v = 5x^2+6xy+5y^2$ with
      $x^2=y^2=xy=0.5$ gives $2.5+3+2.5=8$ — this is $C$'s top eigenvalue, and $(1,1)$ its eigenvector.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Confirm PSD-ness at the minimum-variance direction too">
      $v=(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2},-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2})$ gives $2.5-3+2.5=2$ — still positive. $8$ and $2$ are
      $C$'s two eigenvalues; both positive, so $C$ is (strictly) positive definite.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</WorkedExample>

<Summary>
  $$
  v^\top M v \geq 0 \quad \text{for every vector } v
  $$

  Positive semi-definiteness is what separates "a matrix that could plausibly be a covariance matrix"
  from "a matrix that never could be" — and it reduces entirely to a sign check on eigenvalues. This
  matters beyond statistics: kernel matrices in SVMs, Hessians at a true minimum, and Gram matrices in
  optimization all have to be PSD for the same underlying reason, and the low-rank and SVD chapters
  ahead lean directly on this eigenvalue-sign machinery.
</Summary>
