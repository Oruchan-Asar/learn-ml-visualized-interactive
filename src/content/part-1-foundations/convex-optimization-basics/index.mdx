</Hook>

<Formalize>
  A function is **convex** if the straight line between any two points on it never dips below the curve:

  $$
  f(\theta x + (1-\theta)y) \leq \theta f(x) + (1-\theta)f(y), \qquad \theta \in [0,1]
  $$

  <Variables>
    <Variable>**$x, y$** — any two points in the function's domain.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$\theta$** — a mixing weight between $0$ and $1$, controlling how far between $x$ and $y$ we are.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$f(\theta x + (1-\theta)y)$** — the function's actual value at that in-between point.</Variable>
  </Variables>

  <Steps>
    <Step label="An equivalent one-dimensional test">
      Equivalently, in one dimension: $f''(x) \geq 0$ everywhere.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Why gradient descent's guarantee holds">
      Convexity is the actual reason gradient descent's guarantee holds — **for a convex function, every
      local minimum is automatically the global minimum**, so there's nowhere else downhill could lead.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Non-convex functions have no such guarantee">
      A **non-convex** function can have several local minima, and which one gradient descent finds depends
      entirely on where it starts.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</Formalize>

<WorkedExample>
  Compare $f(x)=x^2$ (a bowl) against $g(x) = x^4 - 4x^2$ (a double well):

  <Steps>
    <Step label="Check convexity with Jensen's inequality">
      For the bowl, between $x=-1$ and $x=1$ (so $\theta=0.5$): the midpoint's value is $f(0)=0$, and the
      average of the endpoints is $0.5(1)+0.5(1)=1$. Since $0 \leq 1$, convexity holds here — and it holds
      everywhere, since $f''(x)=2>0$ always.
    </Step>
    <Step label="The double well fails the same test">
      Its two global minima sit at $x=\pm\sqrt2$, both equal to $-4$ (found by solving
      $g'(x)=4x^3-8x=0 \Rightarrow x=0,\pm\sqrt2$, then $g''(x)=12x^2-8$ confirms $x=0$ is a local *max*
      and $x=\pm\sqrt2$ are minima). Between those two minima ($\theta=0.5$): the midpoint's value is
      $g(0)=0$, but the average of the endpoints is $0.5(-4)+0.5(-4)=-4$. Since $0 > -4$, Jensen's
      inequality is violated — $g$ is not convex, confirmed by the same algebra that found its two minima.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Same algorithm, opposite outcomes">
      Gradient descent from $x=-0.3$ (lr $=0.05$, 40 steps) converges to $x\approx-1.4142$; from
      $x=+0.3$, it converges to $x\approx+1.4142$ — the *other* minimum, despite the two starting points
      being only $0.6$ apart. Convexity is precisely what rules this out.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</WorkedExample>

<ConvexCheckpoint />

<Summary>
  $$
  f(\theta x+(1-\theta)y) \leq \theta f(x)+(1-\theta)f(y) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \text{every local min is the global min}
  $$

  Convexity is the single assumption that turns "gradient descent finds *a* minimum" into "gradient
  descent finds *the* minimum." Most of classical ML's clean optimization guarantees — linear and
  logistic regression's loss, the **SVM (Support Vector Machine)**'s margin problem, ridge regression's closed form — lean on exactly this
  property. Neural network loss landscapes almost never have it, which is why Part III's loss-landscapes
  chapter has to make a very different kind of peace with local minima and saddle points instead.
</Summary>
