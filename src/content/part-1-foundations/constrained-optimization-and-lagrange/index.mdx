<Hook>
  Every optimum so far has been free to sit anywhere. What happens when the answer is required to lie
  exactly on some boundary — a fixed budget, a fixed total — and "downhill" would otherwise walk straight
  off of it?
</Hook>

<Intuition>
  <IntuitionDemo />

  The dashed line is a hard constraint: every point on it satisfies $x+y=4$. The circles are level sets of
  $f(x,y)=x^2+y^2$ — the smaller the circle, the smaller $f$ is there. Slide along the line and watch the
  circle passing through the point grow and shrink.
</Intuition>

<Formalize>
  At the constrained optimum, the objective's level curve is exactly **tangent** to the constraint — their
  gradients point the same direction:

  $$
  \nabla f(x,y) = \lambda\, \nabla g(x,y)
  $$

  <Variables>
    <Variable>**$f(x,y)$** — the objective being minimized (or maximized).</Variable>
    <Variable>**$g(x,y)=0$** — the equality constraint the solution must satisfy.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$\lambda$** — the Lagrange multiplier: the scalar that makes the two gradients equal.</Variable>
  </Variables>

  <Steps>
    <Step label="Why tangency is the condition">
      If the level curve *crossed* the constraint instead of touching it, you could slide a little further
      along the constraint and keep decreasing $f$ — so a crossing point can never be optimal. Only where the
      curve is tangent to the line is there nowhere better left to slide.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Tangent curves share a normal direction">
      Two curves are tangent exactly when their gradients (normal directions) are parallel — which is what
      $\nabla f = \lambda\nabla g$ says algebraically.
    </Step>
    <Step label="λ measures sensitivity to the constraint">
      $\lambda$ isn't just bookkeeping: it says how much the optimal $f$ would change per unit of slack if the
      constraint (here, the "4") were relaxed.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</Formalize>

<Play>
  <PlayDemo />

  The accent arrow is $\nabla f$ at the current point; the dashed-line-colored arrow is the fixed direction
  of $\nabla g=(1,1)$. Slide until they point the same way — that's the only spot on the whole line where
  they agree, and it's exactly where the level circle stops crossing the line and just touches it.
</Play>

<WorkedExample>
  Minimize $f(x,y)=x^2+y^2$ subject to $g(x,y)=x+y-4=0$:

  <Steps>
    <Step label="Write the Lagrange condition componentwise">
      $\nabla f = (2x, 2y)$ and $\nabla g=(1,1)$, so $\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g$ means $2x=\lambda$ and
      $2y=\lambda$ — which forces $x=y$.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Combine with the constraint">
      Substituting $x=y$ into $x+y=4$ gives $2x=4$, so $x=y=2$ and $\lambda=2x=4$.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Check a non-optimal point for contrast">
      At $(0,4)$: $\nabla f=(0,8)$. Matching components gives $\lambda=0$ from $x$ but $\lambda=8$ from $y$ —
      they disagree, so $(0,4)$ is *not* a critical point of the constrained problem, and indeed
      $f(0,4)=16 > f(2,2)=8$.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</WorkedExample>

<LagrangeCheckpoint />

<Summary>
  $$
  \nabla f(x,y) = \lambda\, \nabla g(x,y)
  $$

  Constrained optimization doesn't abandon "follow the gradient" — it just adds one requirement: stay on the
  constraint, and stop only where the objective's gradient has nothing left to offer that the constraint
  would still allow. This exact condition, generalized to inequality constraints via the KKT conditions, is
  what SVMs solve to find their maximum-margin boundary.
</Summary>
