<Hook>
  A distribution has a center and a spread, and two distributions can move together or apart. Three numbers
  capture all of that — but only if you actually accumulate them term by term instead of eyeballing the table.
</Hook>

<Intuition>
  <IntuitionDemo />

  Each row of the table is one outcome; each bar is that outcome's own contribution $p_i \cdot x_i$ (or
  $p_i \cdot y_i$) to the mean. Toggle between $X$ and $Y$ — the bars change, but they always add up to
  exactly the number printed underneath.
</Intuition>

<Formalize>
  The **expectation** (mean) of a discrete random variable weights every outcome by how likely it is:

  $$
  \mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_i p_i\, x_i
  $$

  <Variables>
    <Variable>**$\mathbb{E}[X]$** — the expected value of $X$: its probability-weighted average.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$p_i$** — the probability of outcome $i$.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$x_i$** — the value $X$ takes on outcome $i$.</Variable>
  </Variables>

  **Variance** measures spread around that mean, and **covariance** measures whether two variables move
  together:

  $$
  \mathrm{Var}(X) = \sum_i p_i\,(x_i - \mathbb{E}[X])^2 \qquad
  \mathrm{Cov}(X,Y) = \sum_i p_i\,(x_i - \mathbb{E}[X])(y_i - \mathbb{E}[Y])
  $$

  <Variables>
    <Variable>**$\mathrm{Var}(X)$** — the expected squared deviation of $X$ from its own mean.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)$** — the expected product of $X$'s and $Y$'s deviations from their own means.</Variable>
  </Variables>

  <Steps>
    <Step label="Variance is a special case of covariance">
      $\mathrm{Var}(X) = \mathrm{Cov}(X,X)$ — pairing a variable with itself just squares the deviation.
    </Step>
    <Step label="The sign of covariance is the whole story">
      A positive $\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)$ means $X$ and $Y$ tend to sit on the same side of their means together;
      negative means they tend to sit on opposite sides. The magnitude alone is hard to read without
      normalizing by spread (which is what correlation does).
    </Step>
    <Step label="Every outcome pulls in one direction or another">
      An outcome where both $X$ and $Y$ are above their means, or both below, contributes positively to the
      covariance sum; an outcome where one is above and the other below contributes negatively.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</Formalize>

<Play>
  <PlayDemo />

  Switch between $\mathrm{Var}(X)$, $\mathrm{Var}(Y)$, and $\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)$: the bars are the exact terms
  being summed, and outcome 3 — where $x=3$ sits right at $\mathbb{E}[X]$ — contributes nothing to either
  variance or covariance, because its deviation is zero.
</Play>

<WorkedExample>
  <Steps>
    <Step label="Find E[X] and E[Y]">
      $$
      \mathbb{E}[X] = 0.1(1) + 0.3(2) + 0.2(3) + 0.3(4) + 0.1(5) = 3.0
      $$
      $$
      \mathbb{E}[Y] = 0.1(2) + 0.3(1) + 0.2(4) + 0.3(3) + 0.1(5) = 2.7
      $$
    </Step>
    <Step label="Find Var(X)">
      $$
      \mathrm{Var}(X) = 0.1(1{-}3)^2 + 0.3(2{-}3)^2 + 0.2(0)^2 + 0.3(1)^2 + 0.1(2)^2 = 0.4+0.3+0+0.3+0.4 = 1.4
      $$
    </Step>
    <Step label="Find Cov(X, Y)">
      Pairing each outcome's deviations $(x_i - 3)(y_i - 2.7)$ and weighting by $p_i$:
      $$
      \mathrm{Cov}(X,Y) = 0.1(1.4) + 0.3(1.7) + 0.2(0) + 0.3(0.3) + 0.1(4.6) = 1.2
      $$
      Positive — when $X$ swings away from its mean, $Y$ tends to swing the same direction.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</WorkedExample>

<MomentsCheckpoint />

<Summary>
  $$
  \mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_i p_i x_i \qquad \mathrm{Var}(X) = \sum_i p_i (x_i-\mathbb{E}[X])^2 \qquad
  \mathrm{Cov}(X,Y) = \sum_i p_i (x_i-\mathbb{E}[X])(y_i-\mathbb{E}[Y])
  $$

  These three sums are the entire vocabulary for "where a distribution sits," "how spread out it is," and
  "whether two variables move together" — every model that reports a mean, a variance, or a correlation is
  just this arithmetic in disguise.
</Summary>
