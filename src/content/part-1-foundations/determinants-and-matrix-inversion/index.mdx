<Hook>

  answer a completely different-sounding question: does $Ax=b$ have a unique solution?
</Hook>

<Intuition>
  <IntuitionDemo />

  Drag the sliders. The dashed square is the input; the solid shape is where the matrix sends it. Push
  any slider until the solid shape collapses into a line — that's a singular matrix, and its determinant

</Intuition>

<Formalize>
  For a $2\times2$ matrix, the determinant is:

  $$
  \det(A) = ad - bc
  $$

  <Variables>
    <Variable>**$A$** — the $2\times2$ matrix, with entries $a, b, c, d$ (rows top-to-bottom, columns left-to-right).</Variable>
  </Variables>

  <Steps>
    <Step label="It's a signed area">
      $|\det(A)|$ is the area of the parallelogram the unit square maps to. The sign flips exactly when
      the mapping reverses orientation (flips the plane over).
    </Step>
    <Step label="Zero means singular">
      $\det(A)=0$ exactly when $A$'s two columns point along the same line — the mapped square collapses
      to zero area, and $A$ is **singular**: not invertible.
    </Step>
    <Step label="It gates invertibility">
      $A^{-1}$ exists if and only if $\det(A)\neq0$, since the inverse formula below divides by it.
    </Step>
  </Steps>

  The inverse itself, when it exists:

  $$
  A^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det(A)}\begin{pmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{pmatrix}
  $$

  <Steps>
    <Step label="Why this undoes A">
      $A^{-1}$ is defined precisely so that $A^{-1}A = AA^{-1} = I$ — applying $A$ then $A^{-1}$ (or vice
      versa) leaves every vector exactly where it started.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Singular means no undo">
      If $\det(A)=0$, $A$ has already thrown away a dimension (collapsed the plane to a line) — there's
      no way to recover the lost information, so no inverse can exist.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</Formalize>

<Play>
  <PlayDemo />

  Jump between the invertible preset and the singular one. Watch the inverse readout switch from a
  clean set of numbers to "no inverse" the instant the parallelogram flattens.
</Play>

<WorkedExample>
  For $A = \begin{pmatrix}3&1\\2&2\end{pmatrix}$:

  <Steps>
    <Step label="Compute the determinant">
      $\det(A) = (3)(2) - (1)(2) = 6 - 2 = 4$.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Compute the inverse">
      $A^{-1} = \frac{1}{4}\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\-2&3\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0.5&-0.25\\-0.5&0.75\end{pmatrix}$.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Check it">
      $A A^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix}3&1\\2&2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0.5&-0.25\\-0.5&0.75\end{pmatrix}$.
      Top-left entry: $3(0.5)+1(-0.5)=1.5-0.5=1$. Working through the rest gives exactly
      $\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</WorkedExample>

<DeterminantCheckpoint />

<Summary>
  $$
  \det(A) = ad-bc \qquad\qquad A^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det(A)}\begin{pmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{pmatrix}
  $$

  The determinant is a single number that tells you whether a linear system has a unique solution
  before you do any further work — zero means the matrix has destroyed a dimension, and no amount of
  algebra will get it back. Every later decomposition in this part (eigenvalues, SVD, PSD-ness) leans on
  this same idea: a matrix's determinant is the product of its eigenvalues, so a zero eigenvalue is
  exactly what makes a matrix singular.
</Summary>
