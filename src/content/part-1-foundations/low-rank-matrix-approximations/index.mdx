<Hook>
  A matrix's SVD splits it into a sum of pieces, each carrying a singular value that measures how much
  of the matrix that piece accounts for. What happens if you just... throw away the small pieces?
</Hook>

<Intuition>
  <IntuitionDemo />

  The rank-1 heatmap keeps only the SVD's largest term; rank-2 keeps both and reconstructs the original
  exactly. Watch how close rank-1 already gets, and exactly how large the leftover error is.
</Intuition>

<Formalize>
  The Eckart-Young theorem says the best possible rank-$k$ approximation to $A$ — the one closest to it
  in Frobenius norm, among *every* rank-$k$ matrix — is obtained by keeping only the $k$ largest terms
  of $A$'s SVD:

  $$
  A_k = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \sigma_i u_i v_i^\top \qquad \|A - A_k\|_F = \sqrt{\sum_{i>k} \sigma_i^2}
  $$

  <Variables>
    <Variable>**$\sigma_i$** — the $i$-th singular value of $A$, sorted largest to smallest.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$u_i, v_i$** — the corresponding left and right singular vectors.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$A_k$** — the best possible rank-$k$ approximation of $A$.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$\|\cdot\|_F$** — the Frobenius norm: the square root of the sum of every entry squared.</Variable>
  </Variables>

  <Steps>
    <Step label="Truncation error is exact, not a bound">
      The right-hand equation isn't an upper bound — it's the *exact* error, computable directly from
      the singular values you dropped, without ever forming $A_k$ and subtracting.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Why the small singular values matter least">
      Since the dropped singular values are squared and summed, a handful of small $\sigma_i$'s
      contribute far less error than their count alone would suggest — this is what makes low-rank
      compression work at all.
    </Step>
    <Step label="It's the best rank-k matrix, full stop">
      No other rank-$k$ matrix — found by any method — can get closer to $A$ in Frobenius norm than
      $A_k$ does. Truncating the SVD isn't just convenient; it's provably optimal.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</Formalize>

<Play>
  <PlayDemo />

  $\sigma_1=5$ carries far more of the squared-Frobenius "energy" than $\sigma_2=1$ — which is exactly
  why dropping $\sigma_2$ costs so little.
</Play>

<WorkedExample>
  $A=\begin{pmatrix}3&2\\2&3\end{pmatrix}$ has singular values $\sigma_1=5,\ \sigma_2=1$, with singular
  vectors $u_1=v_1=(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2},\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2})$ and $u_2=v_2=(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2},-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2})$:

  <Steps>
    <Step label="Build the rank-1 approximation">
      $A_1 = 5 u_1 v_1^\top = 5\cdot\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}2.5&2.5\\2.5&2.5\end{pmatrix}$.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Compute the error directly">
      $A - A_1 = \begin{pmatrix}0.5&-0.5\\-0.5&0.5\end{pmatrix}$, and
      $\|A-A_1\|_F=\sqrt{4\times0.5^2}=\sqrt{1}=1$.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Confirm it matches the formula">
      Eckart-Young predicts $\|A-A_1\|_F = \sqrt{\sigma_2^2} = \sigma_2 = 1$ — exactly what direct
      subtraction gave. Retained energy: $\sigma_1^2/(\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2)=25/26\approx96.2\%$.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</WorkedExample>

<LowRankCheckpoint />

<Summary>
  $$
  \|A - A_k\|_F = \sqrt{\sum_{i>k} \sigma_i^2}
  $$

  Compression, denoising, and dimensionality reduction all lean on the same fact: a matrix's
  "important" information concentrates in its largest singular values, and the Eckart-Young theorem
  guarantees that truncating the rest is the best possible rank-$k$ summary you could build. The
  capstone ahead applies exactly this to compress an actual image.
</Summary>
