<Intuition>
  <IntuitionDemo />

  A 4x4 toy "image," reconstructed from 0, 1, or 2 of its singular components. Rank 0 is blank; rank 1
  is already a recognizable (if blurred) sketch; rank 2 is the original back exactly.
</Intuition>

<Formalize>
  Every image matrix $I$ (real or toy) has a singular value decomposition, and its best rank-$k$
  reconstruction is the same truncated-SVD formula from the previous chapter:

  $$
  I_k = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \sigma_i u_i v_i^\top
  $$

  <Variables>
    <Variable>**$I$** — the image, treated as a plain matrix of pixel intensities.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$\sigma_i, u_i, v_i$** — the $i$-th singular value and left/right singular vectors of $I$.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$I_k$** — the rank-$k$ compressed reconstruction of the image.</Variable>
  </Variables>

  <Steps>
    <Step label="What compression actually means here">
      Storing $I_k$ only requires $k$ singular values plus $k$ vectors of each length — for a large
      image, $k$ singular components can cost far fewer numbers than the raw pixel grid.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Where 'eigenfaces' comes from">
      Applied to a collection of face images stacked into one matrix, the same $u_i$ patterns become
      **eigenfaces**: reusable visual building blocks that any face in the set can be approximated from.
    </Step>
    <Step label="The tradeoff is exact, not fuzzy">
      Exactly as in the previous chapter, $\|I - I_k\|_F = \sqrt{\sum_{i>k}\sigma_i^2}$ — you can compute
      precisely how much reconstruction error a given compression ratio costs, before storing anything.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</Formalize>

<Play>
  <PlayDemo />

  Pattern 1 (uniform across every row) alone captures 80% of the image's total energy — a single rank-1
  term already does most of the work.
</Play>

<WorkedExample>
  The toy image is built from two clean row-patterns: $u_1=(0.5,0.5,0.5,0.5)$ with $\sigma_1=8$, and
  $u_2=(0.5,0.5,-0.5,-0.5)$ with $\sigma_2=4$ (each pattern used as both its own left and right singular
  vector, since the image is symmetric):

  <Steps>
    <Step label="Rank-1 reconstruction">
      $I_1 = 8 \cdot u_1u_1^\top$: every entry of $u_1u_1^\top$ is $0.5\times0.5=0.25$, so $I_1$ is a flat
      image with every pixel equal to $8\times0.25=2$.
    </Step>
    <Step label="The error, by direct subtraction">
      $I - I_1$ has entries $\pm1$ in all 16 cells (each of the original's 1's and 3's is exactly 1 away
      from 2). $\|I-I_1\|_F=\sqrt{16\times1^2}=4$.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Confirm it matches Eckart-Young">
      The formula predicts $\|I-I_1\|_F=\sqrt{\sigma_2^2}=\sigma_2=4$ — exactly what direct subtraction
      gave. Energy retained: $8^2/(8^2+4^2)=64/80=80\%$.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</WorkedExample>

<Summary>
  $$
  I_k = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \sigma_i u_i v_i^\top \qquad \|I-I_k\|_F = \sqrt{\sum_{i>k}\sigma_i^2}
  $$

  Image compression, latent feature extraction, and eigenfaces are the same operation wearing different
  names: truncate a matrix's SVD and keep only the directions carrying the most energy. This closes out
  Part II — every idea here (projections, determinants, eigenvalues, PSD-ness, low-rank truncation)
  was building toward this single, reusable move, which reappears throughout the rest of this course
  wherever a large matrix needs to be summarized by a small one.
</Summary>
