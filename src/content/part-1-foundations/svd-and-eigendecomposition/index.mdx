<Hook>
  Chapter 5 showed a matrix stretching, rotating, and flipping a vector. Every direction you dragged got
  bent somewhere else — except, it turns out, two of them. Are there directions a matrix leaves alone?
</Hook>

<Intuition>
  <IntuitionDemo />

  Drag $v$ around. Most of the time, $Av$ points somewhere completely different — the angle between them
  swings all over the place. But two opposite directions are special: keep dragging near the diagonal and
  watch that rotation angle collapse toward $0°$.
</Intuition>

<Formalize>
  A vector $v$ is an **eigenvector** of $A$ if $A$ doesn't rotate it at all — only scales it:

  $$
  Av = \lambda v
  $$

  <Variables>
    <Variable>**$A$** — the matrix being analyzed.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$v$** — the eigenvector: a direction $A$ only scales, never rotates.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$\lambda$** — the eigenvalue: how much that direction gets stretched (or flipped, if negative).</Variable>
  </Variables>

  $\lambda$ is the corresponding **eigenvalue**: how much that direction gets stretched (or, if $\lambda<0$,
  stretched *and flipped*). For a symmetric matrix, the eigenvectors are always perpendicular to each
  other, and $A$ can be rebuilt exactly from them:

  $$
  A = Q\Lambda Q^\top
  $$

  <Variables>
    <Variable>**$Q$** — the matrix whose columns are the (unit) eigenvectors of $A$.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$\Lambda$** — the diagonal matrix holding the eigenvalues, in the same order as $Q$'s columns.</Variable>
  </Variables>

  where $Q$'s columns are the (unit) eigenvectors and $\Lambda$ is diagonal with the eigenvalues on it.
  This is the **eigendecomposition**. The **singular value decomposition (SVD)**, $A = U\Sigma V^\top$,
  is the same idea generalized to *any* matrix, square or not — but its diagonal $\Sigma$ is always
  non-negative, so a negative eigenvalue's direction gets its sign absorbed into $U$ instead.
</Formalize>

<Play>
  <PlayDemo />

  Watch the stretch ratio $|Av|/|v|$ alongside the rotation angle. At the two special directions, the
  ratio locks onto a fixed number — that number *is* $|\lambda|$ for that direction, no matter how far out
  you drag $v$ along it.
</Play>

<WorkedExample>
  For $A = \begin{pmatrix}1&2\\2&1\end{pmatrix}$:

  <Steps>
    <Step label="Solve the characteristic equation">
      $\det(A-\lambda I) = (1-\lambda)^2 - 4 = \lambda^2 - 2\lambda - 3 = (\lambda-3)(\lambda+1) = 0$, so
      $\lambda_1 = 3$ and $\lambda_2 = -1$.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Solve for each eigenvector">
      For $\lambda_1=3$: $(A-3I)v=0 \Rightarrow -2x+2y=0 \Rightarrow x=y$, giving direction $(1,1)$. For
      $\lambda_2=-1$: $(A+I)v=0 \Rightarrow x+y=0$, giving direction $(1,-1)$. Normalized, these are
      $v_1=(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2},\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2})$ and $v_2=(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2},-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2})$ — exactly
      the $45°$ and $-45°$ directions from the demo above.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Turn it into an SVD">
      Singular values are $|\lambda|$, sorted: $\sigma_1=3$, $\sigma_2=1$. Since $\lambda_2$ is negative,
      its left singular vector flips sign: $u_1=v_1$, but $u_2=-v_2$. Reconstructing
      $U\Sigma V^\top = \sigma_1 u_1 v_1^\top + \sigma_2 u_2 v_2^\top$ gives back
      $\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\2&1\end{pmatrix}$ exactly — check it by hand, term by term.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</WorkedExample>

<Summary>
  $$
  Av = \lambda v \qquad\qquad A = Q\Lambda Q^\top \quad(\text{symmetric}) \qquad\qquad A = U\Sigma V^\top \quad(\text{any matrix})
  $$

  Every matrix has a set of directions it only stretches, never rotates — the eigendecomposition finds
  them exactly, by solving one polynomial. This isn't just an algebra exercise: later, Part II's **PCA (Principal Component Analysis)**
  chapter finds "the direction of maximum variance" by eyeballing a scatter plot. That direction is the
  top eigenvector of the data's covariance matrix — this chapter's machinery is what actually computes
  it, instead of eyeballing it.
</Summary>
