import { Hook, Intuition, Formalize, Play, WorkedExample, Steps, Step, Summary } from "@/components/chapter/Beats";
import { IntuitionDemo, PlayDemo, GradientCheckpoint } from "./Demos";

<Hook>
  You know how to find downhill when there's one direction to move in. What does "downhill" even mean once there's an $x$ *and* a $y$?
</Hook>

<Intuition>
  <IntuitionDemo />

  This is a bowl-shaped landscape seen from above — brighter means lower. Drag the point around. There's no single tangent line anymore, but there's still an arrow pointing the steepest way out.
</Intuition>

<Formalize>
  That arrow is the **gradient** — now a vector, one component per input:

  $$
  \nabla f(x,y) = \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x},\ \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\right)
  $$

  Each partial derivative is computed exactly like Chapter 1's derivative — just holding the *other* variable fixed. For $f(x,y) = x^2 + y^2$: treating $y$ as a constant, $\partial f/\partial x = 2x$; treating $x$ as a constant, $\partial f/\partial y = 2y$.
</Formalize>

<Play>
  <PlayDemo />

  Drag the point and watch both components change together, and the arrow's length track the gradient's magnitude — longer means steeper.
</Play>

<WorkedExample>
  <Steps>
    <Step label="Compute each partial derivative at (3, 4)">
      $$
      \nabla f = (2 \times 3,\ 2 \times 4) = (6, 8)
      $$
    </Step>
    <Step label="Compute the gradient's magnitude">
      $$
      |\nabla f| = \sqrt{6^2+8^2} = \sqrt{100} = 10
      $$

      The same 3:4:5 triangle from the vectors chapter, showing up again.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</WorkedExample>

<Summary>
  $$
  \nabla f(x,y) = \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x},\ \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\right)
  $$

  In multiple dimensions, the gradient is a vector of partial derivatives — one per input — and it still points in the direction of steepest increase, zeroing out exactly at a flat spot like a minimum.
</Summary>
