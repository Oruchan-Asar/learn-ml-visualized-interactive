<Hook>
  Q-learning discovers a policy indirectly — learn a value for every action, then always take the best one. What if a policy could just be a set of parameters, adjusted by gradient ascent, the same way every other model in this course has been?
</Hook>

<Intuition>
  <IntuitionDemo />

  Back to the three-armed bandit — but instead of a Q-table, there's a **preference** number for each arm, run through a softmax to get a probability of picking it. Step through five pulls and watch the bars themselves shift: an arm that pays off gets *more* likely to be picked next time, one that doesn't gets less so.
</Intuition>

<Formalize>
  With preferences $\theta$ and policy $\pi_\theta(a) = \text{softmax}(\theta)_a$, the **REINFORCE** update after taking action $a$ and observing return $G$ is:

  $$
  \theta_i \leftarrow \theta_i + \alpha\big(\mathbb{1}[i{=}a] - \pi_\theta(i)\big)\,G
  $$

  Look closely at $\big(\mathbb{1}[i{=}a] - \pi_\theta(i)\big)$ — it's *exactly* the softmax cross-entropy gradient from Part III, indicator minus predicted probability. The only difference is what it's scaled by: cross-entropy always scales by $1$ (the true label is certain), while REINFORCE scales by $G$, whatever return that action happened to earn. A big positive return pushes hard toward that action; a return of exactly zero pushes not at all.
</Formalize>

<Play>
  <PlayDemo />

  Compare the very first policy to the one after five pulls. Nothing here ever built or consulted a value table — the probabilities shifted directly, one gradient step at a time, purely from the returns each pull happened to produce.
</Play>

<WorkedExample>
  Starting from $\theta=(0,0,0)$ — a uniform policy — with $\alpha=0.5$:

  <Steps>
    <Step label="First pull: arm A, reward 1">
      $\pi = (\frac13,\frac13,\frac13)$ before the update. $\theta_A \leftarrow 0 + 0.5(1-\frac13)(1) = \frac13$;
      both $\theta_B$ and $\theta_C$ drop to $-\frac16$ — pushed down even though they weren't pulled, simply
      for not being the chosen action.
    </Step>
    <Step label="A reward of exactly zero changes nothing">
      Pulling arm C next happens to pay $0$. Since the update scales by the return, multiplying by $0$
      zeroes out every single term — $\theta$ comes out of that step completely unchanged, regardless of
      which arm was pulled.
    </Step>
    <Step label="After all five pulls">
      $\theta_B \approx 0.498$, clearly ahead of $\theta_A \approx -0.074$ and $\theta_C \approx -0.424$ — the
      policy now assigns arm B *more than half* its probability mass, without ever computing a single
      $Q(s,a)$.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</WorkedExample>

<Summary>
  $$
  \theta_i \leftarrow \theta_i + \alpha\big(\mathbb{1}[i{=}a] - \pi_\theta(i)\big)\,G
  $$

  Q-learning and policy gradients solve the exact same problem from two different directions: one estimates *values* and reads a policy off them; the other adjusts a policy *directly*, using nothing but the returns actions happen to produce. Both are, underneath, still gradient-based learning from a numeric signal — the same idea as every optimizer since Part II, aimed at a different kind of objective. The next chapter applies that same direct-policy idea somewhere it's become extremely consequential: teaching a language model to prefer the answers people actually like.
</Summary>
