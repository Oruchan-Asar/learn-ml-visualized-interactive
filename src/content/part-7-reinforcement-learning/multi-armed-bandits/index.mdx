<Hook>
  Every model in this course so far learned from a fixed dataset that already existed before training started. What if the only way to get more data was to *act* — and every action costs you the chance to have tried something else instead?
</Hook>

<Intuition>
  <IntuitionDemo />

  Three arms, three unknown reward rates. Pull one — its estimate updates. Pull it again, and it either supports or undermines what you just learned. There's no dataset to hand you upfront here; the only way to find out anything is to spend a pull finding out.
</Intuition>

<Formalize>
  Each arm's estimated value is just the running average of what it's paid out so far:

  $$
  \hat{Q}(a) = \frac{1}{n_a}\sum_{i=1}^{n_a} r_i, \qquad \text{greedy choice} = \underset{a}{\arg\max}\; \hat{Q}(a)
  $$

  <Variables>
    <Variable>**$\hat{Q}(a)$** — the running-average estimate of arm $a$'s value, updated after every pull.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$a$** — one of the arms (actions) available to pull.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$n_a$** — the number of times arm $a$ has been pulled so far.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$r_i$** — the reward received on the $i$-th pull of that arm.</Variable>
  </Variables>

  A purely greedy policy — always pull whichever arm currently looks best — can get stuck: an early lucky (or unlucky) run of pulls can lock in a wrong estimate before enough evidence comes in to correct it. **Epsilon-greedy** fixes this with the simplest possible patch: most of the time pull the current best arm (*exploit*), but some fraction of the time pull a different one anyway, just to keep checking (*explore*).
</Formalize>

<Play>
  <PlayDemo />

  Step through a fixed 8-pull run. The first three pulls explore all three arms once each — arm A and arm B both come back showing $1.0$, a tie. From here, a purely greedy policy could pull only A forever and never find out B is actually the better arm.
</Play>

<WorkedExample>
  Arms A, B, C have fixed (but hidden from the policy) true means $0.5$, $0.8$, $0.2$. The script explores A, B, C once each, then exploits:

  <Steps>
    <Step label="After the first three explore steps">
      A$=[1]\to1.0$, B$=[1]\to1.0$, C$=[0]\to0.0$ — A and B are tied for best, purely by chance on a single
      pull each.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Exploiting the tie">
      Ties break alphabetically, so the policy exploits A next: A$=[1,0]\to0.5$. Now B is the clear leader
      and gets exploited: B$=[1,1]\to1.0$.
    </Step>
    <Step label="One more explore step saves it">
      A scheduled explore step revisits A a third time: A$=[1,0,1]\to0.667$. Two more exploit steps follow,
      landing on B$=[1,1,0]\to0.667$ — tied with A again, by coincidence, after just 8 pulls total.
    </Step>
  </Steps>

  Even this short run shows the whole tension: without that one extra explore step, the policy might have kept exploiting A on a stale, lucky-looking estimate.
</WorkedExample>

<BanditsCheckpoint />

<Summary>
  $$
  \hat{Q}(a) = \frac{1}{n_a}\sum_{i=1}^{n_a} r_i
  $$

  A bandit is reinforcement learning with everything stripped away except the one tension that defines the whole field: acting changes what you know, and what you know should change how you act. There's no state to track yet — every pull starts from the same "state" the last one did. The next chapter adds exactly that: a world where the action you take also decides *what situation you're in next*.
</Summary>
