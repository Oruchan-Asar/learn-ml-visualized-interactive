<Hook>
  Every pull of a bandit's arm started the same way the last one did — there was no "situation" to carry from one pull to the next. What happens the moment an action doesn't just pay a reward, but also decides where you are for the *next* decision?
</Hook>

<Intuition>
  <IntuitionDemo />

  A row of four states, the last one a goal. Move left or right — each move costs $-1$, except the one that lands you on the goal, which pays $+10$. Unlike the bandit, your position now depends on every choice you've made so far, and every future choice depends on where those moves left you.
</Intuition>

<Formalize>
  A **Markov decision process** is the tuple $(S, A, P, R, \gamma)$ — states, actions, a transition rule, a reward function, and a discount factor. For a fixed policy $\pi$, the **value** of a state is its expected discounted future reward, defined recursively by the **Bellman equation**:

  $$
  V^\pi(s) = R(s, \pi(s)) + \gamma\, V^\pi\big(P(s,\pi(s))\big)
  $$

  <Variables>
    <Variable>**$V^\pi(s)$** — the value of state $s$ under policy $\pi$: its expected discounted future reward.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$s$** — the current state, one of the situations the agent can be in.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$\pi(s)$** — the policy: which action it prescribes from state $s$.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$R(s, \pi(s))$** — the reward function: the immediate reward for taking that action in that state.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$\gamma$** — the discount factor, shrinking the value of reward that takes longer to arrive.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$P(s,\pi(s))$** — the transition rule: the state landed in after taking that action.</Variable>
  </Variables>

  <Steps>
    <Step label="The same idea as the chain rule">
      This is the exact same idea as Part I's chain rule — the value of *right now* depends on the value of
      *whatever comes next* — just unrolled over an action-dependent sequence of states instead of a fixed
      chain of functions.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</Formalize>

<Play>
  <PlayDemo />

  Every state's value is highest closest to the goal and lower further away — not because those states are inherently better, but because $\gamma < 1$ discounts reward that takes longer to arrive. The value function is entirely a consequence of the policy and the discount, not something separately assigned to each state.
</Play>

<WorkedExample>
  With $\gamma = 0.9$, under the policy "always move right":

  <Steps>
    <Step label="Start from the goal and work backward">
      $V(3) = 0$ (nothing left to earn). $V(2) = 10 + 0.9(0) = 10$ — one move from $+10$.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Two states from the goal">
      $V(1) = -1 + 0.9(10) = 8$. $V(0) = -1 + 0.9(8) = 6.2$.
    </Step>
    <Step label="A policy that never arrives">
      Under "always move left," any state above $0$ eventually gets stuck bouncing at the boundary forever,
      each bounce costing $-1$. Solving $V = -1 + 0.9V$ for a fixed point gives
      $V = -1/(1-0.9) = -10$ — a genuinely worse value than even the very first state gets under "always
      right."
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</WorkedExample>

<Summary>
  $$
  V^\pi(s) = R(s, \pi(s)) + \gamma\, V^\pi\big(P(s,\pi(s))\big)
  $$

  Every value in this chapter came from a policy that was already fixed and handed to you — "always right" was simply given, not learned. The real question reinforcement learning exists to answer is the one this chapter sidesteps: which policy should an agent follow, when nobody tells it in advance? The next chapter is the first algorithm that actually learns the answer.
</Summary>
