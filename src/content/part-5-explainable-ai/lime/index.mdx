<Hook>
  Saliency maps and Grad-CAM both needed to reach inside a convolutional network's own structure. What explains a prediction from a model with no gradients to inspect at all — a random forest, a rules engine, anything you can only query, never open up?
</Hook>

<Intuition>
  <IntuitionDemo />

  The curve is a "black box" — LIME never sees its formula, only its outputs. Drag the query point $x_0$: LIME samples a few points near it, weights the close ones more than the far ones, and fits the simplest possible model — a straight line — through just that weighted neighborhood.
</Intuition>

<Formalize>
  Around a query point $x_0$, take samples $x_i$ nearby, weight each by a kernel that favors closeness, and fit a weighted linear regression:

  $$
  w_i = \exp\!\left(-\frac{(x_i-x_0)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right), \qquad \hat y = \text{slope}\cdot x + \text{intercept}
  $$

  where the slope and intercept minimize $\sum_i w_i(y_i - \hat y_i)^2$ — an ordinary least-squares fit, just with each point's error scaled by how close it is to $x_0$. Nothing here needs the black box's internals: LIME only ever calls it as a function, exactly like a saliency map does, but a linear fit instead of a per-input gradient.
</Formalize>

<Play>
  <PlayDemo />

  Drag $x_0$ anywhere and compare LIME's local slope to the curve's actual derivative at that point, $2x_0$ — a fact LIME itself never gets to see, since it only ever queries the black box, never its formula. Watch how closely a *local*, linear approximation can still track a genuinely *curved* function, as long as it's never asked to explain anywhere but right around $x_0$.
</Play>

<WorkedExample>
  The black box computes $f(x)=x^2$ (unknown to LIME). At $x_0=3$, with kernel width $\sigma=1.5$, LIME samples $x \in \{1,2,3,4,5\}$, giving $y \in \{1,4,9,16,25\}$:

  <Steps>
    <Step label="Fit the weighted local line">
      Solving the weighted least-squares equations gives slope $=6$, intercept $\approx -7.57$.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Compare to the true derivative">
      $f'(x) = 2x$, so $f'(3)=6$ — LIME's local slope matches the exact derivative, even though LIME never
      computed a derivative at all, only fit a line through five sampled points.
    </Step>
    <Step label="But the line doesn't pass through (3, 9)">
      At $x=3$ the fitted line gives $6(3)-7.57 \approx 10.43$, not $9$. LIME's local model tracks the
      *slope* faithfully — that's what makes it a good explanation of sensitivity — but it's a regression
      through a neighborhood, not an interpolation pinned to the exact point.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</WorkedExample>

<Summary>
  $$
  w_i = \exp\!\left(-\frac{(x_i-x_0)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right), \qquad \hat y = \text{slope}\cdot x + \text{intercept}
  $$

  LIME's entire trick is scope: don't explain the whole model, just the tiny neighborhood around one prediction, where almost anything looks roughly linear. That's also its limitation — the same black box gets a *different* local explanation at every point you ask about, and none of them say anything reliable once you're far from where they were fit. The next two chapters take a different approach: a single, game-theoretically justified way to split credit among features, valid everywhere at once.
</Summary>
