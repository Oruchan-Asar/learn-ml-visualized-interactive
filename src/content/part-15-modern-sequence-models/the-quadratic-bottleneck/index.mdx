<Hook>
  Part IV's self-attention let every token look at every other token, all at once — no fixed window, no
  recency bias. That power has a price nobody mentioned yet: what happens to that cost as the sequence
  gets long?
</Hook>

<Intuition>
  <IntuitionDemo />

  Attention's cost isn't just "bigger" than a linear layer's — it's a completely different *shape* of
  bigger. Double the sequence length and the linear layer's cost doubles too. Attention's quadruples.

</Intuition>

<Formalize>
  Self-attention builds an $n \times n$ score matrix — every one of $n$ queries compared against every one
  of $n$ keys, each comparison a $d$-dimensional dot product:

  $$
  \text{attention ops} = n^2 d, \qquad \text{linear-layer ops} = nd, \qquad \frac{\text{attention ops}}{\text{linear-layer ops}} = n
  $$

  That last ratio is the whole chapter: attention costs exactly $n$ times more than a single per-token
  operation, where $n$ is the sequence length *itself*. The embedding dimension $d$ changes the raw
  numbers, but it cancels out of the ratio completely — it's the sequence length alone that decides how
  much worse attention gets.
</Formalize>

<Play>
  <PlayDemo />

  The dashed line is the linear layer's cost; the bold curve is attention's. They start close together at
  small $n$ and pull apart fast — by $n=128$, attention has left the line so far behind that the line is
  barely visible near the bottom of the chart. That gap is the entire reason the rest of this part exists.
</Play>

<WorkedExample>
  Fixing $d=8$ and doubling the sequence length twice, starting from $n=8$:

  <Steps>
    <Step label="n = 8">
      Attention: $8^2 \times 8 = 512$ ops. Linear layer: $8 \times 8 = 64$ ops. Attention costs $8\times$
      as much.
    </Step>
    <Step label="n = 16 (doubled once)">
      Attention: $16^2 \times 8 = 2{,}048$ ops — exactly $4\times$ the $n=8$ value. Linear layer:
      $16 \times 8 = 128$ ops — exactly $2\times$. Attention now costs $16\times$ as much, not $8\times$.
    </Step>
    <Step label="n = 32 (doubled again)">
      Attention: $32^2 \times 8 = 8{,}192$ ops — $4\times$ the previous step, again. Linear layer:
      $256$ ops — $2\times$ again. The ratio has doubled to $32\times$. Every doubling of $n$ doubles the
      ratio too, because the ratio *is* $n$.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</WorkedExample>

<BottleneckCheckpoint />

<Summary>
  $$
  \frac{\text{attention ops}}{\text{linear-layer ops}} = n
  $$

  A Transformer processing a 512-token paragraph pays a manageable quadratic cost. The same architecture
  on a 100,000-token codebase or a book-length context pays a cost that's grown by a factor of nearly 200
  relative to the paragraph case, purely from the ratio $n$ itself growing. That single number — not a
  bug, not a bad implementation, just what $n^2$ does — is the reason an entire research area exists
  around finding attention alternatives that don't pay it. The next chapter looks at the first one: what
  if the softmax inside attention were swapped for something that let the whole computation be
  rearranged into a running sum instead of a full matrix?
</Summary>
