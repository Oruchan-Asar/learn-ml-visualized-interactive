<Hook>
  The last chapter ended on a shift-invariant relative bias — a lookup table keyed by distance, bolted
  onto the score after the fact. What if position could fall out of the dot product itself, with no
  separate bias term and no embedding ever added to the content? Rotate the query and key instead of
  translating them, and it does.
</Hook>

<Intuition>
  <IntuitionDemo />

  The key sits fixed at position 0. Step the query's position up and its arrow spins around the origin —
  the vector's *length* never changes, only its *angle*. Watch the dot product: it isn't tracking where the
  query "is" in any absolute sense, it's tracking how far its angle has swung away from the key's.
</Intuition>

<Formalize>
  RoPE rotates a 2D pair of coordinates by an angle proportional to position, before the dot product is
  ever taken:

  $$
  R(pos)\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}\cos(pos\cdot\theta) & -\sin(pos\cdot\theta)\\ \sin(pos\cdot\theta) & \cos(pos\cdot\theta)\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix}
  $$

  <Variables>
    <Variable>**$pos$** — the token's position in the sequence.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$\theta$** — the fixed angular rate: how many radians the pair rotates per position step.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$R(pos)$** — the rotation matrix applied to that coordinate pair before the dot product.</Variable>
  </Variables>

  <Steps>
    <Step label="Rotation preserves length, and preserves relative angle">
      $R$ is orthogonal: it never stretches a vector, only turns it. Turning two vectors by the *same*
      angle leaves the angle between them — and so their dot product — completely unchanged.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Turning them by different amounts is the same as turning one by the difference">
      $R(a)q \cdot R(b)k = q \cdot R(b-a)k$. The rotated dot product between a query at position $a$ and a
      key at position $b$ is identical to rotating the key alone by $b-a$ and leaving the query untouched.
      Only the *difference* of the two positions ever enters the calculation.
    </Step>
    <Step label="A real model repeats this across many frequency pairs">
      Full RoPE splits the embedding into $d/2$ coordinate pairs, each with its own $\theta$ — exactly the
      geometric spacing of frequencies from the sinusoidal scheme, just used to rotate instead of to add.
      This demo uses one pair to keep every angle exact and checkable by hand.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</Formalize>

<Play>
  <PlayDemo />

  Slide the query's position continuously. The key stays anchored at position 0, so the angle swept by the
  slider *is* the relative distance — and the dot product it produces depends on nothing else. At $\theta =
  \pi/2$, four steps is a full turn: the vector — and the dot product — returns exactly to where it started
  every four positions.
</Play>

<WorkedExample>
  $Q_0=(1,\,0.5)$, $K_0=(0.5,\,1)$, $\theta = \pi/2$ (a quarter turn per position step):

  <Steps>
    <Step label="Distance 0: the plain dot product">
      $Q_0\cdot K_0 = 1(0.5) + 0.5(1) = 1.0$. With no rotation at all, this is just the ordinary dot
      product.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Distance 2: rotate the key by a half turn">
      $R(\pi)K_0 = (-0.5,\,-1)$. Then $Q_0\cdot R(\pi)K_0 = 1(-0.5)+0.5(-1) = -1.0$.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Same distance, different absolute positions — identical answer">
      Query at position 1, key at position 3 (still 2 apart): $R(\pi/2)Q_0=(-0.5,\,1)$,
      $R(3\pi/2)K_0=(1,\,-0.5)$. Their dot product: $(-0.5)(1)+(1)(-0.5) = -1.0$ — exactly matching the
      distance-2 score computed from positions 0 and 2.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</WorkedExample>

<RopeCheckpoint />

<Summary>
  $$
  R(a)q \cdot R(b)k = q \cdot R(b-a)k
  $$

  Rotating a query and key by an angle proportional to position makes their dot product depend only on
  the *difference* of the two angles — the relative distance between them — never on either position by
  itself. That's relative position falling directly out of the geometry, with no added embedding and no
  separate bias table to look up. It's also exactly why RoPE runs into trouble past the sequence lengths
  it was trained on: push $pos\cdot\theta$ far enough and the model is rotating into angles it never saw
  during training. The next chapter is about stretching that trained range without retraining at all.
</Summary>
