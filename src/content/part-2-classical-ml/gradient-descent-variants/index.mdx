<Intuition>

<Formalize>
  That's **batch** gradient descent — the gradient is the average over every example:

  $$
  \nabla L_{\text{batch}} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \nabla L_i
  $$

  <Variables>
    <Variable>**$\nabla L_{\text{batch}}$** — the batch gradient: the direction used for one step, averaged over the whole dataset.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$n$** — the total number of training examples.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$\nabla L_i$** — the gradient of the loss computed from a single example $i$.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$i$** — the index of one training example.</Variable>
  </Variables>

  <Steps>
    <Step label="Stochastic gradient descent">
      Uses just one example per step, $\nabla L_i$ for a single $i$ — cheap, but noisy.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Mini-batch gradient descent">
      Splits the difference, averaging over a small subset $B$: $\frac{1}{|B|}\sum_{i \in B} \nabla L_i$.
    </Step>
    <Step label="All three trade accuracy for cost">
      Batch, stochastic, and mini-batch are all estimates of the same true gradient — they just trade accuracy
      per step for cost per step.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</Formalize>

<Play>
  <PlayDemo />

  Switch modes and take a few steps each time. Batch heads straight for the minimum. Stochastic zigzags — each step only "knows about" one point, so it's regularly a little wrong — while mini-batch sits somewhere in between.
</Play>

<WorkedExample>
  At $(w,b)=(0,0)$:

  <Steps>
    <Step label="Compute one point's stochastic gradient">
      For squared-error loss, each point's gradient is $(\partial L_i/\partial w, \partial L_i/\partial b) =
      (-2x_i r_i,\ -2r_i)$, where $r_i=y_i-(wx_i+b)$ is that point's residual. At $(w,b)=(0,0)$, the point
      $(-2,-3.2)$ has residual $r=-3.2-0=-3.2$, giving:

      $$
      (-2(-2)(-3.2),\ -2(-3.2)) = (-12.8,\ 6.4)
      $$
    </Step>
    <Step label="Compare it to the full batch gradient">
      At the same $(w,b)=(0,0)$, every residual equals $y_i$ (the prediction is $0$ everywhere). Each
      point's $(-2x_iy_i,\ -2y_i)$ pair:
      - $x=-2,y=-3.2$: $(-12.8,\ 6.4)$
      - $x=-1,y=-0.8$: $(-1.6,\ 1.6)$
      - $x=0,y=1.3$: $(0,\ -2.6)$
      - $x=1,y=3.1$: $(-6.2,\ -6.2)$
      - $x=2,y=4.7$: $(-18.8,\ -9.4)$

      Averaging each column: $w$-gradient $=(-12.8-1.6+0-6.2-18.8)/5=-7.88$, $b$-gradient
      $=(6.4+1.6-2.6-6.2-9.4)/5=-2.04$ — same rough neighborhood as the single point above, but a
      different $w$-magnitude and even a *flipped sign* on $b$.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Average over a full pass">
      The individual gradients exactly reconstruct the batch gradient — noise that cancels out over time, not bias.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</WorkedExample>

<VariantsCheckpoint />

<Summary>
  $$
  \nabla L_{\text{batch}} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \nabla L_i, \qquad \nabla L_{\text{stochastic}} = \nabla L_i
  $$

  Every step is still exactly Chapter 2's descent rule from Part I — only *which examples* compute the gradient changes. Real training almost always uses mini-batches: batch is often too slow to even compute once, and pure stochastic is noisier than necessary.
</Summary>
