<Hook>
  Two predictors that move together almost perfectly — a company's revenue in dollars and in thousands
  of dollars, say — shouldn't confuse a model. But ordinary least squares can hand one of them a huge
  positive weight and the other a huge negative one, and still call it the "best" fit. What's going on,
  and how do you stop it?
</Hook>

<Intuition>
  <IntuitionDemo />

  These two predictors are correlated at nearly 0.998 — practically the same signal, twice. At λ=0, watch
  the fitted weights: one strongly positive, one strongly negative, even though both features move with
  the target in the same direction. Push λ up and watch that flip resolve into two ordinary, positive
  weights instead.
</Intuition>

<Formalize>
  Ridge regression adds a penalty on the size of the weights themselves to the squared-error loss being minimized:

  $$
  \text{SSE}(\mathbf{w}) + \lambda \sum_j w_j^2
  $$

  <Variables>
    <Variable>**SSE($\mathbf{w}$)** — the sum of squared errors of the fit, same as ordinary least squares.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$\lambda$** — the penalty strength. $\lambda=0$ recovers plain OLS; larger $\lambda$ shrinks weights harder.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$w_j$** — the model's $j$-th weight (the intercept, if there is one, is conventionally left unpenalized).</Variable>
  </Variables>

  Minimizing this is still a quadratic problem, so it still has a closed form — just with $\lambda$ added to
  the diagonal of the normal equations: $(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X} + \lambda I)\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}$.

  <Steps>
    <Step label="Collinearity makes X^TX nearly singular">
      When two predictors are highly correlated, $\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}$ is close to singular — its
      inverse blows up, and tiny changes in the data produce wildly different "optimal" weights.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Adding λ to the diagonal fixes that directly">
      Adding $\lambda$ to every diagonal entry pulls $\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X} + \lambda I$ away from
      singular, however close the raw predictors are to collinear — the matrix stays invertible and the
      solution stays finite and stable.
    </Step>
    <Step label="With correlated predictors, ridge splits credit evenly">
      Ridge has no way to prefer one of two near-identical predictors over the other — its penalty treats
      them symmetrically — so as $\lambda$ grows it pushes their weights toward being *equal*, sharing the
      job between them instead of letting one dominate.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</Formalize>

<Play>
  <PlayDemo />

  Watch the sum of squared errors (SSE) as you move λ: it barely changes, even while the two weights swing
  from wildly different to nearly identical. Near-perfect collinearity means many different weight
  combinations fit the data almost equally well — OLS just happens to land on an unstable one.
</Play>

<WorkedExample>
  Two predictors, $x_1 = [1,2,3,4]$ and $x_2 = [1,2,3,4.5]$ — correlated at $\approx 0.998$ — fit to
  $y=[2,4,7,8]$ with no intercept.

  <Steps>
    <Step label="Unregularized: an unstable, sign-flipped fit">
      Solving the plain normal equations gives $w_1 \approx 3.929$, $w_2 \approx -1.714$. Both predictors
      rise together, yet one gets a strongly negative weight — a symptom of collinearity, not a real
      pattern in the data.
    </Step>
    <Step label="A modest penalty (λ = 1) already fixes the sign">
      $(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X} + 1 \cdot I)\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}$ solves to
      $w_1 \approx 1.116$, $w_2 \approx 0.887$ — both positive, and much closer together.
    </Step>
    <Step label="A strong penalty (λ = 5) makes them nearly equal">
      At $\lambda=5$: $w_1 \approx 0.940$, $w_2 \approx 0.941$ — ridge has split the credit between the two
      near-identical predictors almost exactly evenly, exactly as the theory predicts.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</WorkedExample>

<RidgeCheckpoint />

<Summary>
  $$
  \text{SSE}(\mathbf{w}) + \lambda \sum_j w_j^2, \qquad (\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}+\lambda I)\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{y}
  $$

  Ridge regression's job is stability, not sparsity: by adding $\lambda$ to the diagonal of the normal
  equations, it keeps a near-singular system invertible and turns wildly unstable, sign-flipped weights
  into a sane, shared split of credit among correlated predictors.
</Summary>
