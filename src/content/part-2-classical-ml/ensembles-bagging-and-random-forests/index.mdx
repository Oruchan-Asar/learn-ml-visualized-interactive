<Hook>
  Last chapter, pruning a tree back was the only way to stop it from memorizing noise. What if you didn't have to prune at all?
</Hook>

<Intuition>
  <IntuitionDemo />

  Every tree here is grown to the same reckless max depth as Chapter 8's worst offender — each one individually overfits. But each is trained on a different random resample of the data, so each one overfits *differently*. Add more of them and watch the jagged, noise-chasing regions smooth out into the one boundary they all actually agree on.
</Intuition>

<Formalize>
  This is **bagging** — bootstrap aggregating. Train $N$ trees, each on a random sample drawn *with replacement* from the training set (a "bootstrap sample"), then combine their predictions by majority vote:

  $$
  \hat{y}(x) = \text{majority}\big(T_1(x), T_2(x), \ldots, T_N(x)\big)
  $$

  <Variables>
    <Variable>**$\hat{y}(x)$** — the ensemble's final prediction for input $x$.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$T_i(x)$** — the prediction of the $i$-th tree in the forest.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$N$** — the number of trees in the ensemble.</Variable>
    <Variable>**majority** — the vote rule: whichever label the most trees predicted wins.</Variable>
  </Variables>

  Each $T_i$ still overfits its own bootstrap sample. But the noise it overfits to is specific to *which* points that resample happened to include — different resamples chase different noise, so it rarely lines up. A **random forest** adds one more trick on top: at every split, each tree only gets to consider a random subset of the available features. With just one feature here, that trick has nothing to act on — bagging alone is already the whole story.
</Formalize>

<Play>
  <PlayDemo />

  Watch training accuracy stay roughly flat while validation accuracy climbs as trees are added. No single tree in the forest got any less overfit — the ensemble generalizes better anyway, purely because their individual mistakes don't overlap.
</Play>

<WorkedExample>
  At $x = 14.25$ (true label B):

  <Steps>
    <Step label="Vote with the first three trees">
      B, A, A — majority vote says **A**, wrong.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Add two more trees">
      Predicting B and B, the vote among all five becomes B, A, A, B, B — majority **B**, correct.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Notice what actually changed">
      No tree changed its mind; two more independent (wrong-in-different-ways) opinions were enough to outvote the
      two that agreed on the mistake.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</WorkedExample>

<BaggingCheckpoint />

<Summary>
  $$
  \hat{y}(x) = \text{majority}\big(T_1(x), T_2(x), \ldots, T_N(x)\big)
  $$

  Bagging doesn't fix any individual tree's tendency to overfit — it cancels it out. As long as each tree's mistakes are driven by *different* noise, averaging enough of them together drowns the noise out and leaves the shared signal standing.
</Summary>
