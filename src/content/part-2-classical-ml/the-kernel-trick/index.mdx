import { Hook, Intuition, Formalize, Play, WorkedExample, Steps, Step, Summary, Variables, Variable } from "@/components/chapter/Beats";
import { IntuitionDemo, PlayDemo, KernelCheckpoint } from "./Demos";

<Hook>
  Last chapter's line found the widest possible street between two classes. What happens when there's no street at all — no line, at any angle, separates them?
</Hook>

<Intuition>
  <IntuitionDemo />

  One class rings the origin; the other rings that ring. Drag the line anywhere — there's always at least one point on the wrong side. This isn't a bad line; it's that *no* line can do better than about two-thirds here, because a straight cut can't tell "close to the center" from "far from it."
</Intuition>

<Formalize>
  The data isn't separable in $(x, y)$ — but it doesn't have to stay in $(x, y)$. Lift every point through one new feature, the squared distance from the origin:

  $$
  z = x^2 + y^2
  $$

  <Variables>
    <Variable>**$z$** — the new lifted feature: a point's squared distance from the origin.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$x$, $y$** — the point's original two coordinates.</Variable>
  </Variables>

  <Steps>
    <Step label="Radius collapses points regardless of angle">
      Points near the center now share one small value of $z$; points on the outer ring share one large value,
      *no matter which direction they're in*.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Angle was the confusion, radius was the signal">
      The two classes were tangled by angle but separated by radius all along — $z$ just throws away the angle
      and keeps the radius.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</Formalize>

<Play>
  <PlayDemo />

  This is the exact same 16 points, plotted by $z$ instead of $(x,y)$. One threshold now separates them completely — it's Chapter 7's split all over again, just on a feature that didn't exist until this chapter lifted it into being.
</Play>

<WorkedExample>
  <Steps>
    <Step label="Transform an inner and an outer point">
      $(1.8, 0)$: $z = 1.8^2 = 3.24$. $(4.7, 0)$: $z = 4.7^2 = 22.09$.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Transform a diagonal point at the same inner radius">
      $(1.556, 1.556)$: $z = 1.556^2 + 1.556^2 \approx 2.42+2.42 = 4.84$ — the *same* value every inner point at
      that radius gets, regardless of angle.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Confirm a single threshold separates everything">
      Every inner point lands with $z \le 4.84$; every outer point lands with $z \ge 22.09$. Any threshold in
      between — 10, 15, 20 — separates all sixteen points at once.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</WorkedExample>

<KernelCheckpoint />

<Summary>
  $$
  z = x^2 + y^2
  $$

  This is the kernel trick's whole idea: a problem that's hopeless in the original features can become trivial in a cleverly chosen new one. Real kernels (polynomial, **RBF** — short for **Radial Basis Function**) do this implicitly, for far higher-dimensional lifts than one extra coordinate — but the shape of the win is exactly what you just saw: unseparable becomes separable, and the same one-threshold or widest-street machinery from the last two chapters still does the rest.
</Summary>
