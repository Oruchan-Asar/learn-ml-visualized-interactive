<Hook>
  RLVR's reward only looks at the FINAL answer — correct or not, after the whole reasoning chain is
  already written. That's late feedback: a promising-looking first step can lead nowhere, and nothing
  catches it until the very end. What if each individual step could be scored on its own?
</Hook>

<Intuition>
  <IntuitionDemo />

  A reasoning tree: 3 possible first steps, each branching into 2 possible second steps. Step through
  each branch and see the two numbers that make up a path's score — a **Process Reward Model** (PRM)
  score for each individual step, not just one score for the finished answer.
</Intuition>

<Formalize>
  A path's overall score is the PRODUCT of its per-step PRM scores — exactly like chaining conditional
  probabilities:

  $$
  \text{score}(\text{path}) = \prod_{t} \text{PRM}(\text{step}_t)
  $$

  <Variables>
    <Variable>**$\text{score}(\text{path})$** — the overall score for one complete root-to-leaf reasoning path.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$\text{PRM}(\text{step}_t)$** — the process reward model's score for step $t$: how likely this partial derivation is to still be on track to a correct final answer.</Variable>
  </Variables>

  <Steps>
    <Step label="Scoring steps, not just outcomes">
      An Outcome Reward Model (ORM) — RLVR's verifier from the previous chapter — only ever sees the
      finished response. A PRM scores every intermediate step, giving a signal *before* the reasoning is
      finished.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Per-step scores enable a search, not just a rating">
      Once every candidate step has its own score, a search procedure can branch: expand several
      candidate next steps, score each with the PRM, and keep pursuing only the promising ones —
      instead of committing to one linear chain and hoping.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Full-path evaluation beats greedy, step-by-step commitment">
      A greedy search that locks in whichever first step scores highest, then only looks at that
      branch's children, can miss the actual best path entirely — evaluating full paths (as a real tree
      search does) is what catches that.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</Formalize>

<Play>
  <PlayDemo />

  Branch A's first step scores highest in isolation ($0.9$) — the most tempting one to commit to
  greedily. But its best second step only reaches $0.3$, for a final path score of just $0.27$: the
  WORST of the three branches. Branch C's first step looks weakest ($0.35$) yet leads to an excellent
  second step ($0.95$), landing the best overall path at $0.3325$. A search that only looked one step
  ahead would never have found it.
</Play>

<WorkedExample>
  The full tree, all six paths:

  <Steps>
    <Step label="Branch A (first-step score 0.9 — the tempting one)">
      A→A1: $0.9 \times 0.3 = 0.27$. A→A2: $0.9 \times 0.2 = 0.18$. Best in this branch: $0.27$.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Branch B (first-step score 0.5)">
      B→B1: $0.5 \times 0.6 = 0.30$. B→B2: $0.5 \times 0.4 = 0.20$. Best in this branch: $0.30$.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Branch C (first-step score 0.35 — the weakest-looking one)">
      C→C1: $0.35 \times 0.2 = 0.07$. C→C2: $0.35 \times 0.95 = 0.3325$. Best in this branch: $0.3325$
      — the best path in the ENTIRE tree, despite starting from the lowest first-step score of the
      three branches.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</WorkedExample>

<Summary>
  $$
  \text{score}(\text{path}) = \prod_{t} \text{PRM}(\text{step}_t)
  $$

  A Process Reward Model turns "is this answer right?" into "is this STEP still on track?" — a signal
  available before the reasoning chain is even finished. That per-step signal is what makes tree search
  over reasoning paths possible at all, and it's what catches exactly the failure mode this chapter's
  tree demonstrated: the step that looks most promising in isolation isn't always the one that leads
  anywhere good. The next chapter turns back to alignment training itself, with two DPO variants that
  each drop one of DPO's own requirements.
</Summary>
