<Hook>
  A bigger feedforward layer usually means a slower one — every token has to pass through every parameter. What if a model could have dozens of feedforward blocks, each specialized, and every token only ever paid the cost of the two or so that actually matter for it?
</Hook>

<Intuition>
  <IntuitionDemo />

  Four small experts, one router. Pick a token and watch which two experts light up — the other two contribute nothing to the output and never even get evaluated.
</Intuition>

<Formalize>
  A **mixture-of-experts** layer replaces one feedforward block with $N$ of them plus a router. The router scores every expert, keeps only the top $K$, renormalizes their scores to sum to 1, and combines just those:

  $$
  y = \sum_{i \in \text{top-}K(\text{router}(x))} \frac{p_i}{\sum_{j \in \text{top-}K} p_j} \cdot \text{Expert}_i(x)
  $$

  Every expert outside the top $K$ contributes exactly zero — not a small weight, but literally never computed. A model can have far more total parameters than a dense one while running only a fraction of them per token.
</Formalize>

<Play>
  <PlayDemo />

  Token $x=2$ and token $x=-2$ activate completely disjoint pairs of experts — zero overlap. Each token gets routed to whichever specialists the router thinks fit it best, and the rest of the model, for that token, might as well not exist.
</Play>

<WorkedExample>
  Four experts, router weights $[0.5, -0.5, 1.0, -1.0]$, top-2 routing:

  <Steps>
    <Step label="Token x=2 routes to experts 2 and 0">
      Router logits: $[1, -1, 2, -2]$. Softmax gives expert 2 the highest probability, expert 0 second. Their
      renormalized weights: $\approx0.731$ and $\approx0.269$. Experts 1 and 3 are never evaluated.
    </Step>
    <Step label="The combined output blends only the selected two">
      Expert 2's output is $1$, expert 0's is $2$. Combined: $0.731(1) + 0.269(2) \approx 1.269$ — a weighted
      blend of exactly two specialists, not all four.
    </Step>
    <Step label="A different token activates a disjoint pair entirely">
      Token $x=-2$ flips every router logit's sign, routing instead to experts 3 and 1 — the exact two experts
      that sat idle for $x=2$. Same router, same experts, completely different active subset.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</WorkedExample>

<MoECheckpoint />

<Summary>
  $$
  y = \sum_{i \in \text{top-}K} \tilde p_i \cdot \text{Expert}_i(x) \qquad\text{— total capacity can grow, per-token compute doesn't have to}
  $$

  Mixture-of-experts decouples a model's total parameter count from its per-token compute cost: adding more experts adds capacity, but as long as $K$ stays fixed, every token still only touches $K$ of them. That's the entire mechanism behind how modern large language models scale to hundreds of billions of parameters while keeping inference cost close to a much smaller dense model's. The next chapter asks the question this raises directly: given a compute budget, how should it actually be spent — more parameters, or more data?
</Summary>
