<Hook>
  Training a model 10x larger, just to see if it's worth it, can cost more than the entire rest of a project. What if four small, cheap training runs could tell you almost exactly what the expensive one would have shown, before you ever ran it?
</Hook>

<Intuition>
  <IntuitionDemo />

  The same four training runs, fit two different ways. One method transforms the data before fitting a line; the other fits the raw numbers directly. Only one of them produces a sane prediction at a size fifty times bigger than anything actually trained.
</Intuition>

<Formalize>
  Loss often follows a **power law** in model size: $L(N) = A \cdot N^{-\alpha}$. Taking logs turns this into something linear:

  $$
  \log L = \log A - \alpha \log N
  $$

  <Variables>
    <Variable>**$L$** (or $L(N)$) — the model's loss at a given size.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$N$** — the model size (number of parameters).</Variable>
    <Variable>**$A$** — a constant scale factor in the power law, fit from data.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$\alpha$** — the scaling exponent: how fast loss falls as $N$ grows.</Variable>
  </Variables>

  <Steps>
    <Step label="Log-log OLS recovers the law directly">
      Fitting ordinary least squares to $(\log N, \log L)$ recovers $\alpha$ and $A$ directly — the exact same
      OLS machinery from Part IX, just applied after a log transform.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Fitting the raw numbers assumes the wrong shape">
      Fitting a line to the *raw* $(N, L)$ pairs instead assumes a linear relationship that was never true,
      and extrapolates accordingly.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</Formalize>

<Play>
  <PlayDemo />

  Four training runs at sizes 1, 4, 9, and 16 — and a fifth point at $N=100$, never trained, just computed from the true law for comparison. All five points sit exactly on one straight line in log-log space, because that line *is* the power law.
</Play>

<WorkedExample>
  True law $L(N) = 100 \cdot N^{-0.5}$, observed at $N \in \{1, 4, 9, 16\}$ giving losses $\{100, 50, 33.\overline{3}, 25\}$:

  <Steps>
    <Step label="Log-log OLS recovers the law exactly">
      Fitting $\log L$ against $\log N$ gives slope $\approx-0.500$ and intercept $\approx\ln(100)$ — both
      matching the true law to full floating-point precision, since the training data has no noise at all.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Extrapolating to N=100: almost exactly right">
      Plugging $N=100$ into the fitted log-log line and exponentiating back gives a predicted loss of
      $\approx10.000$ — matching the true value of exactly $10$, despite $N=100$ being far outside the training
      range of $1$ to $16$.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Fitting the raw numbers instead: a nonsensical answer">
      A straight line fit directly to $(N, L)$ — no log transform — predicts a loss of $\approx-351.26$ at
      $N=100$. A negative loss is meaningless; the raw-linear model was extrapolating a relationship that was
      never actually linear.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</WorkedExample>

<Summary>
  $$
  \log L = \log A - \alpha \log N \qquad\text{— the same line, just plotted on the axes the relationship actually lives on}
  $$

  A scaling law isn't a prediction trick specific to language models — it's ordinary least squares, applied after recognizing that a power-law relationship becomes linear in log space. Get the transform right, and four cheap runs can extrapolate to a size fifty times larger with almost no error; get it wrong, and the same four runs produce a nonsensical negative loss. The next chapter turns from what to train to how to use a model once it's trained: getting useful behavior out of a frozen model through the prompt alone, no gradient updates at all.
</Summary>
