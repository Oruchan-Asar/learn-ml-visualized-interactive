<Hook>
  RLVR and GRPO both spend compute at TRAINING time — sampling groups, verifying them, updating
  weights. But a model that has already finished training can still trade accuracy for compute at
  the moment you actually ask it something: think longer before answering, at zero cost to the
  weights themselves.
</Hook>

<Intuition>
  <IntuitionDemo />

  Drag the chain-of-thought length and watch accuracy climb along the curve — steeply at first,
  then flattening out. Every extra reasoning step still helps, but each one recovers a shrinking
  slice of the remaining gap to a perfect score.
</Intuition>

<Formalize>
  Accuracy approaches, but never reaches, a ceiling as the chain of thought gets longer — a
  geometric decay toward $1$:

  $$
  \text{accuracy}(L) = 1 - (1 - a_0)\,d^{\,L}
  $$

  <Variables>
    <Variable>**$L$** — chain-of-thought length, in reasoning steps, spent before committing to an answer.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$a_0$** — the single-shot accuracy at $L=0$, with no reasoning steps at all.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$d$** — the decay factor: the fraction of the remaining gap to perfect accuracy left AFTER one more step. $d < 1$ means each step still helps, but by a shrinking amount.</Variable>
  </Variables>

  <Steps>
    <Step label="Diminishing, not disappearing, returns">
      $d^L \to 0$ as $L$ grows, so accuracy keeps climbing forever — but $d^L$ shrinks
      geometrically, so each additional step buys less than the one before it.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Compute cost is a different shape entirely">
      Accuracy is concave in $L$ (bending downward), but the compute spent generating that chain of
      thought is linear in $L$ — a straight line, no diminishing anything.
    </Step>
    <Step label="A concave gain against a linear cost has an optimal stopping point">
      Net utility (accuracy gained minus compute spent) rises while the marginal accuracy gain
      still exceeds the constant marginal cost, then falls once it doesn't — that crossing point is
      the compute-optimal chain-of-thought length.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</Formalize>

<Play>
  <PlayDemo />

  Drag again, but now watch three numbers at once: accuracy climbing, compute cost climbing right
  alongside it in a straight line, and net utility rising, peaking, then falling. Past the peak,
  every extra reasoning step is spending more compute than the accuracy it buys is worth.
</Play>

<WorkedExample>
  Base accuracy $a_0 = 0.2$, decay $d = 0.8$, cost $0.02$ compute units per reasoning step:

  <Steps>
    <Step label="Accuracy at a few chain-of-thought lengths">
      - $L=0$: $1 - 0.8 \times 0.8^0 = 1 - 0.8 \times 1 = 0.2$
      - $L=1$: $1 - 0.8 \times 0.8^1 = 1 - 0.64 = 0.36$
      - $L=4$: $0.8^4 = 0.4096$, so $1 - 0.8 \times 0.4096 = 1 - 0.32768 \approx 0.672$

      Each step's gain is smaller than the last.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Compute cost grows linearly">
      - $L=5$ costs $0.02 \times 5 = 0.10$ units.
      - $L=10$ costs exactly twice that, $0.20$ units.

      No discount for going further.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Net utility peaks at L=10">
      Utility $= \text{accuracy}(L) - 0.02L$ at the three candidate lengths:

      - $L=9$: $0.8^9\approx0.1342$, accuracy $=1-0.8\times0.1342\approx0.8926$, utility
        $\approx0.8926-0.02\times9=0.8926-0.18=0.7126$
      - $L=10$: $0.8^{10}\approx0.1074$, accuracy $=1-0.8\times0.1074\approx0.9141$, utility
        $\approx0.9141-0.02\times10=0.9141-0.20=0.7141$
      - $L=11$: $0.8^{11}\approx0.0859$, accuracy $=1-0.8\times0.0859\approx0.9313$, utility
        $\approx0.9313-0.02\times11=0.9313-0.22=0.7113$

      $L=10$'s utility ($\approx0.7141$) beats both neighbors — the compute-optimal chain-of-thought
      length for this cost weight, found by the same search a real inference-time compute budget
      would run.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</WorkedExample>

<Summary>
  $$
  \text{accuracy}(L) = 1 - (1 - a_0)\,d^{\,L}
  $$

  Test-time compute is a second knob, entirely separate from training: the same trained policy can
  spend more or less inference-time compute per query and trade latency for accuracy on the fly,
  with no gradient step involved. Diminishing returns mean there's always a compute-optimal chain
  length past which more reasoning steps cost more than they're worth — exactly the tradeoff a
  deployed reasoning model has to navigate for every single request.
</Summary>
