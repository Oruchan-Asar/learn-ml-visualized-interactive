</Hook>

<Formalize>
  **GRPO** (short for **Group Relative Policy Optimization**) samples a GROUP of $G$ responses to the
  same prompt, then normalizes each one's reward against that group's own statistics:

  $$
  A_i = \frac{r_i - \text{mean}(r_1, \dots, r_G)}{\text{std}(r_1, \dots, r_G)}
  $$

  <Variables>
    <Variable>**$A_i$** — the advantage assigned to sampled response $i$.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$r_i$** — response $i$'s own reward (e.g. RLVR's binary verifiable reward from the previous chapter).</Variable>
    <Variable>**$\text{mean}(r_1,\dots,r_G)$, $\text{std}(r_1,\dots,r_G)$** — the mean and standard deviation of all $G$ rewards in the group.</Variable>
  </Variables>

  <Steps>
    <Step label="The baseline comes from the samples themselves">
      PPO's baseline is a learned prediction of expected return. GRPO's baseline is just the group's
      own mean — no network, no separate training signal, no value-function bias to worry about.
    </Step>
    <Step label="The scale comes from the samples too">
      Dividing by the group's standard deviation keeps the advantage's scale comparable across prompts
      of very different difficulty:
      - A group where everyone agrees produces small, tightly-scaled advantages
      - A group with wildly different rewards produces larger ones
    </Step>
    <Step label="No critic network, anywhere">
      That's GRPO's entire pitch: PPO's clipped policy-gradient update, unchanged, but fed an advantage
      computed in closed form from one batch of samples instead of a trained critic's guess.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</Formalize>

<Play>
  <PlayDemo />

  Same formula, two different groups. On the easy problem, four of five responses are correct — being
  the one that's wrong is expensive: advantage $-2.0$, far below the four correct responses' modest
  $+0.5$ each. On the hard problem, only one of five is correct — now being right is what's rare and
  valuable: advantage $+2.0$, while each of the four wrong responses only drops to $-0.5$. The exact
  same rewards, interpreted relative to two different groups, produce two very different training
  signals.
</Play>

<WorkedExample>
  Five sampled responses to an easy problem, rewards $[1, 1, 1, 1, 0]$ — four correct, one wrong:

  <Steps>
    <Step label="Group statistics">
      - Mean $= (1+1+1+1+0)/5 = 4/5 = 0.8$
      - This is a Bernoulli group, so variance $= p(1-p) = 0.8 \times 0.2 = 0.16$
      - $\text{std} = \sqrt{0.16} = 0.4$
    </Step>
    <Step label="Advantage for a correct response">
      $A = (1 - 0.8)/0.4 = 0.2/0.4 = 0.5$.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Advantage for the one wrong response">
      $A = (0 - 0.8)/0.4 = -0.8/0.4 = -2.0$ — four times the magnitude of the reward for getting it
      right, purely because being wrong was rare in this group.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</WorkedExample>

<Summary>
  $$
  A_i = \frac{r_i - \text{mean}(r_1, \dots, r_G)}{\text{std}(r_1, \dots, r_G)}
  $$

  GRPO's entire innovation is replacing a trained critic's baseline with one computed directly from a
  group of samples — mean for the baseline, standard deviation for the scale. It's the exact
  combination that made scaling RLVR to reasoning models practical: no second network to train and
  keep in sync, just statistics over whatever batch of responses was already sampled. The next chapter
  goes one level deeper than a single final-answer reward: scoring each individual reasoning STEP, not
  just the finished response.
</Summary>
