<Hook>
  Part XVIII's RLHF chapter fit a reward model because nobody could write down "this answer is worth
  7.3 points" for an open-ended response. But a math problem has exactly one right number. Why train a
  fallible proxy to guess at something you can just *check*?
</Hook>

<Intuition>
  <IntuitionDemo />

  Four sampled responses to one arithmetic problem. Step through them — each one gets checked against
  the true answer directly, no reward model involved anywhere in the loop.
</Intuition>

<Formalize>
  RLVR's reward is the verifier's own output, nothing learned in between:

  $$
  r(y) = \mathbb{1}[\,\text{verify}(y) = \text{correct}\,]
  $$

  <Variables>
    <Variable>**$r(y)$** — the reward assigned to sampled response $y$.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$\text{verify}(y)$** — a program that checks $y$ against ground truth: exact-match on a final answer, or a test suite that a piece of code either passes or fails.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$\mathbb{1}[\cdot]$** — the indicator function: $1$ if the condition holds, $0$ otherwise.</Variable>
  </Variables>

  <Steps>
    <Step label="No reward model to fit, no reward model to fool">
      RLHF's reward model has to be trained on preference data and can be exploited by any response
      that merely *looks* convincing to it. A verifier that checks an exact answer or runs a test suite
      has no such surface to attack.
    </Step>
    <Step label="The reward is binary and exact">
      Every sampled response gets exactly $0$ or $1$ — no in-between score, no calibration to worry
      about, no drift between the reward model and the policy being trained against it.
    </Step>
    <Step label="This only works where a verifier exists">
      RLVR is not a universal replacement for RLHF — open-ended writing has no ground-truth checker.
      It's the right tool specifically for math, code, and anything else with a checkable answer.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</Formalize>

<Play>
  <PlayDemo />

  The verifiable reward and the learned reward model's guesses average out to almost the same number
  across these four responses. But look at which SPECIFIC response each one likes best: the verifier
  correctly picks a response that got the right answer. The learned reward model — scoring based on
  what *sounds* plausible — picks a response that's flatly wrong. That's reward hacking in miniature,
  and it's exactly the failure mode a verifiable reward can't have.
</Play>

<WorkedExample>
  Four sampled responses to "What is 17 + 26?" (correct answer: $43$):

  <Steps>
    <Step label="Verify each response exactly">
      - "$43$" → correct → reward $1$
      - "$39$" → wrong → reward $0$
      - "$43$" → correct → reward $1$
      - "$44$" → wrong → reward $0$

      Mean verifiable reward: $(1+0+1+0)/4 = 0.5$.
    </Step>
    <Step label="Compare against the learned reward model's guesses">
      Guesses of $0.62, 0.71, 0.55, 0.30$ for those same four responses, in order. Sum:
      $0.62+0.71+0.55+0.30=2.18$. Mean: $2.18/4 = 0.545$ — close to the verifier's $0.5$ on average.
    </Step>
    <Step label="But the rankings disagree where it matters">
      The learned RM's highest guess ($0.71$) belongs to "$39$" — the WRONG answer. The verifier would
      never make that mistake: it isn't guessing, it's checking.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</WorkedExample>

<Summary>
  $$
  r(y) = \mathbb{1}[\,\text{verify}(y) = \text{correct}\,]
  $$

  RLVR replaces a learned proxy for correctness with correctness itself, wherever a verifier can be
  written. It's not a fix for RLHF's reward model — it's a sidestep, available only on the subset of
  tasks (math, code, anything checkable) where "correct" isn't a matter of preference at all. The next
  chapter asks a harder question: once responses are sampled and scored this way, how does the policy
  actually turn that binary signal into a training update — without training a separate critic network
  to estimate a baseline?
</Summary>
