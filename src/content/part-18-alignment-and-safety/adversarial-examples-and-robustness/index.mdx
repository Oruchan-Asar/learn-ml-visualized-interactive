<Formalize>
  **FGSM** (Fast Gradient Sign Method) perturbs every input dimension by a fixed budget $\epsilon$, in
  whichever direction most decreases the classifier's score — for a linear model, that direction is just
  the negative sign of each weight:

  $$
  x'_i = x_i - \epsilon \cdot \text{sign}(w_i), \qquad \Delta(w \cdot x) = -\epsilon \sum_i |w_i|
  $$

  <Variables>
    <Variable>**$x_i$** — the original value of the $i$-th input dimension (one pixel).</Variable>
    <Variable>**$x'_i$** — that same dimension's value after the perturbation is applied.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$\epsilon$** — the fixed perturbation budget applied to every dimension.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$w_i$** — the classifier's weight on dimension $i$.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$\Delta(w \cdot x)$** — the resulting shift in the classifier's weighted sum (its score) once every dimension has been nudged.</Variable>
  </Variables>

  Each individual $|x'_i - x_i|$ is bounded by $\epsilon$ — small, imperceptible on its own. But the total
  shift in the dot product the classifier actually uses scales with the *number of dimensions*, not with
  $\epsilon$ alone. A perturbation invisible in any single pixel becomes a large, deliberate shift once
  summed across all of them.
</Formalize>

<Play>
  <PlayDemo />

  Confidence falls steadily as epsilon grows — not a single dramatic jump, a gradual decline that
  eventually crosses 0.5. The classifier isn't confused about any individual pixel; it's just adding up
  nine small, coordinated pushes in exactly the wrong direction.
</Play>

<WorkedExample>
  9 pixels at 0.6, weights all 1, bias $-4.5$:

  <Steps>
    <Step label="Before any perturbation">
      $w \cdot x + b = 9(0.6) - 4.5 = 0.9$. $\sigma(0.9) \approx 0.711$ — confidently positive.
    </Step>
    <Step label="At epsilon = 0.1">
      Every pixel drops to $0.5$. $w \cdot x + b = 9(0.5) - 4.5 = 0$. $\sigma(0) = 0.5$ — sitting exactly
      on the boundary.
    </Step>
    <Step label="At epsilon = 0.15">
      Every pixel drops to $0.45$ — barely different from $0.5$. $w \cdot x + b = 9(0.45) - 4.5 = -0.45$.
      $\sigma(-0.45) \approx 0.389$ — now confidently classified in the *other* class.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</WorkedExample>

<Summary>
  $$
  x' = x - \epsilon \cdot \text{sign}(w), \qquad \Delta(w\cdot x) = -\epsilon\sum_i |w_i|
  $$

  This chapter's classifier was linear and its weights were visible — real adversarial attacks on deep
  networks use the same sign-of-gradient idea, computed via backpropagation through the whole network
  instead of read directly off a weight vector, which is exactly why adversarial examples generalize
  across architectures the attacker never even saw. Robustness training (adding perturbed examples like
  these directly into the training set) is one real defense; it trades some clean accuracy for resistance
  to exactly this kind of engineered nudge. The next chapter looks at a related but distinct failure: not
  a classifier fooled by pixels, but a language model talked past its own safety training by phrasing
  alone.
</Summary>
