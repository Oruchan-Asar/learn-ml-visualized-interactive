<Hook>
  Part VII's **RLHF** — short for **Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback** — chapter fit a reward
  model from preference comparisons, then ran a separate
  policy-gradient loop against it — two stages, two different update rules. What if the algebra let both
  stages collapse into one?
</Hook>

<Intuition>
  <IntuitionDemo />

  Apply each comparison. The policy shifts after every single one — no reward model sits in between the
  preference data and the policy update at all.
</Intuition>

<Formalize>
  For an optimal policy under a **KL** (**Kullback–Leibler**)-regularized reward objective, the reward and the policy are related in
  closed form: $r(y) = \beta \log\big(\pi(y)/\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)\big) + \text{const}$. Substitute that
  directly into the Bradley-Terry preference loss from Part VII, and the reward model cancels out
  algebraically, leaving a loss purely in terms of the policy:

  $$
  \mathcal{L} = -\log\sigma\Big(\beta\big[(\log\pi(y_w) - \log\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w)) - (\log\pi(y_l) - \log\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l))\big]\Big)
  $$

  <Variables>
    <Variable>**$\mathcal{L}$** — the DPO loss being minimized for a single preference pair.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$\beta$** — the KL-penalty strength inherited from the RLHF objective; controls how far the policy is allowed to drift from the reference.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$\pi$** — the policy currently being trained.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$\pi_{\text{ref}}$** — the frozen reference policy (typically the pre-RLHF model) the trained policy is measured against.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$y_w$** — the winning (preferred) response in the comparison.</Variable>
    <Variable>**$y_l$** — the losing (dispreferred) response in the comparison.</Variable>
  </Variables>

  <Steps>
    <Step label="The reward model's term cancels in the gradient">
      Differentiating this with respect to the policy's own parameters, the softmax's $\pi(a)$ term
      cancels *exactly* between the winner and loser gradients.
    </Step>
    <Step label="What's left is a single-stage update">
      One gradient step per preference pair, straight on the policy — no reward model and no separate RL
      update rule required, simpler than RLHF's two-stage procedure.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</Formalize>

<Play>
  <PlayDemo />

  Two completely different training procedures — fit-a-reward-model-then-run-RL versus one direct
  gradient loop — landing on the same ranking from the same three comparisons. Neither path is "more
  correct"; **DPO** — short for **Direct Preference Optimization** — is just a shortcut through algebra
  that happens to skip a stage RLHF needs.
</Play>

<WorkedExample>
  The very first comparison, "B beats A", starting from a uniform policy:

  <Steps>
    <Step label="At initialization, the policy IS the reference">
      $\pi = \pi_{\text{ref}}$ everywhere, so $\log\pi(y_w)-\log\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w) = 0$ for every arm.
      The bracketed term is $0 - 0 = 0$, so $h=\beta \cdot 0 = 0$ and $\sigma(0)=0.5$.
    </Step>
    <Step label="The gradient step">
      The update is $\theta \to \theta + \eta\,(1-\sigma(h))\,\beta\,(\mathbb{1}[a{=}y_w]-\mathbb{1}[a{=}y_l])$,
      with learning rate $\eta=0.5$ and $\beta=0.5$. With $\sigma(h)=0.5$, the update factor
      $(1-\sigma(h))=0.5$ pushes $\theta_B$ up and $\theta_A$ down by the same amount:

      - $\theta_B \to 0 + \underbrace{0.5}_{\eta}\underbrace{(0.5)}_{1-\sigma(h)}\underbrace{(0.5)}_{\beta}(1) = 0.125$
      - $\theta_A \to 0 - 0.125 = -0.125$
    </Step>
    <Step label="Nothing else moved">
      $\theta_C$ is untouched — this comparison never mentioned C, and DPO's update (like RLHF's) only
      touches the two arms actually being compared.
    </Step>
  </Steps>
</WorkedExample>

<DPOCheckpoint />

<Summary>
  $$
  \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L} = -\big(1-\sigma(h)\big)\,\beta\,\big(\mathbb{1}[a{=}y_w] - \mathbb{1}[a{=}y_l]\big)
  $$

  DPO isn't a different objective from RLHF — it's the *same* Bradley-Terry preference objective, with
  the reward model's role folded into the policy's own log-ratio against a reference. That's the entire
  appeal: one training loop instead of two, no separate reward model to overfit or drift out of sync with
  the policy being trained against it. The next chapter asks where the preference *data* itself comes
  from in the first place — what happens when a model critiques its own answers instead of a human
  supplying every comparison.
</Summary>
